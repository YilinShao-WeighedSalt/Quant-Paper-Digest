1. The Right Wing of the SABR Smile with an Absorbing Boundary: Resolution of Conjectures of Henry-Labordère

Zeyu Cao, Shaosai Huang
math.PR · 2026-10-03 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

A famous rule-of-thumb for how far "deep out-of-the-money" option prices decay was secretly wrong whenever the market's price and its jitteriness move in opposite directions — and this paper finds the corrected formula.

Plain English

The problem. An option is a bet that a price will finish above (or below) some level. The further that level is from today's price, the less likely the bet pays, so the option gets cheaper. Traders summarize an option's price through its implied volatility — roughly, how jumpy the market thinks the asset will be. Plot implied volatility against how far out-of-the-money the option is and you get a curve called the volatility smile. The far right edge of that curve — the "right wing" — controls the price of very optimistic bets, and getting it wrong means mispricing tail risk, the exact scenario that blows up trading desks.

Everyone uses a workhorse model called SABR to draw this smile. Back in the 2000s, the quant Pierre Henry-Labordère conjectured a clean formula for where the right wing should flatten out as you go infinitely far out-of-the-money. People have used variants of that intuition ever since.

The idea. This paper asks: is that conjectured limit actually correct? The twist is that SABR has a correlation knob, $\rho$, controlling whether the price and its own volatility tend to move together or apart. The authors show the answer depends entirely on the sign of $\rho$. When price and volatility move together ($\rho \ge 0$), the old conjecture holds. But when they move in opposite directions ($\rho < 0$) — which is the normal case in equity markets, where crashes come with volatility spikes — the conjecture fails, and the true wing is steeper than everyone assumed.

The intuition for why is lovely. To reach a very high price, the asset needs a long lucky run of high volatility. But if price and volatility are negatively correlated, that lucky volatility run keeps trying to drag the price back down. There's a second, independent source of randomness that has to thread a needle — stay above a moving barrier — just to let the price survive the journey upward. That extra constraint costs probability, so extreme bets are even less likely than the old formula said.

What they found. They pinned down the exact decay rate for every correlation value, proved Henry-Labordère's first conjecture true for $\rho \ge 0$ and false for $\rho < 0$, and supplied the correct replacement: the decay is governed by a distance measured inside the survival region rather than the naive straight-line distance.

The Math

The setup. SABR models a forward price $F_t$ and its stochastic volatility $\alpha_t$ by $$dF_t = \alpha_t F_t^{\beta}\, dW_t, \qquad d\alpha_t = \nu\, \alpha_t\, dZ_t, \qquad d\langle W, Z\rangle_t = \rho\, dt,$$ with absorption imposed at $F = 0$ (once the price hits zero it stays). Here $F_t$ is the forward price, $\alpha_t$ the volatility, $\beta \in (0,1)$ the elasticity controlling how volatility scales with price level, $\nu > 0$ the "vol-of-vol," and $W_t, Z_t$ are Brownian motions with correlation $\rho \in (-1,1)$.

The key result. Let $$P(k) = \mathbb{P}\big(F_T \ge f_0 e^{k}\big)$$ be the probability the terminal forward exceeds its start $f_0$ by a factor $e^k$. The paper proves the large-$k$ tail rate $$-\,\frac{1}{k^2}\ln P(k) \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{(1-\beta)^2}{2\,\nu^2\,T\,\big(1-(\rho\wedge 0)^2\big)} \quad\text{as } k\to\infty,$$ where $T$ is maturity, $\rho\wedge 0 = \min(\rho,0)$, and $\ln$ is the natural log. Because the implied volatility's wing slope is read off this rate, it follows that the Black–Scholes implied volatility tends to $$\sigma_{\text{BS}}(k) \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\nu\sqrt{1-(\rho\wedge 0)^2}}{1-\beta}.$$ For $\rho \ge 0$ this collapses to Henry-Labordère's conjectured $\nu/(1-\beta)$; for $\rho < 0$ the extra factor $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$ makes the wing strictly higher.

The method. The proofs are "direct" — no heat-kernel expansions. They apply a Lamperti transform (a change of variable turning $F^\beta$ dynamics into something with constant diffusion), then a conditional time change that freezes the volatility path, reducing the survival question to a Gaussian process kept above a moving square-root barrier. Sharp Gaussian moving-barrier estimates plus change-of-measure (Girsanov) arguments bound the probability from both sides. The matching upper and lower bounds give the exact $k^2$ rate.

Results with numbers. The headline is structural rather than a benchmark table: the corrected rate is larger by the factor $1/(1-\rho^2)$ precisely when $\rho < 0$, and the limiting wing volatility is scaled up by $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$. The second Henry-Labordère conjecture — that the rate equals a squared unrestricted Gaussian distance — is shown to fail for $\rho<0$ and is replaced by the squared intrinsic Riemannian distance within the survival domain from start state to target set.

Jargon Decoded

  • Implied volatility — the single volatility number that, plugged into Black–Scholes, reproduces an option's market price.
  • SABR model — a standard stochastic-volatility model where both the forward and its volatility are random and correlated.
  • Absorbing boundary — a rule that once the price touches zero it is stuck there forever, affecting how tails behave.
  • Lamperti transform — a substitution that rescales a process so its random part has constant size, simplifying analysis.
  • Girsanov / change of measure — a technique for re-weighting probabilities so a hard-to-study path becomes typical under a new, tractable probability.
  • Riemannian distance — the length of the shortest path between two states when "length" is defined by the model's own geometry, not straight-line coordinates.

Honest Take

This is a rigorous asymptotic ($k\to\infty$) result, so it describes the far wing; real options trade at finite strikes where sub-leading corrections still matter, and the clean limit may not be reached at liquid maturities. It also lives inside the SABR idealization (one volatility factor, absorption at zero), which real markets only approximate. But the qualitative lesson — that negative price–vol correlation steepens the true wing — is a genuine correction practitioners should heed when extrapolating smiles.

Bottom Line

When volatility rises as prices fall, the far edge of the option smile is steeper than the textbook formula claimed — and now we know exactly how much.

2. A posteriori error bounds for the Uncertain Volatility Model

Lokman A Abbas-Turki, Chassagneux Jean-Fran{\c c}ois, Jean-Philippe Lemor, Gr{é}goire Loeper, Simon Sananes
math.OC · 2026-10-05 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When you can only guess an option's price with a computer approximation, this paper hands you a rigorous "the true answer is somewhere between here and here" guarantee — even when you refuse to pin down the volatility.

Plain English

The problem. To price an option you normally need to know the asset's volatility — how wildly it swings. But nobody knows future volatility; you only have a range, say "somewhere between 15% and 35%." The Uncertain Volatility Model (UVM) embraces that honesty: instead of one number, it prices the option against the worst case volatility inside your range, giving a conservative price a cautious trader can trust. The catch is that computing this worst-case price requires solving a hard nonlinear equation, so in practice you approximate it — with a neural network, a policy-gradient scheme, whatever. And then the nagging question: how wrong is my approximation? Without an answer, a "price" is just a number you hope is close.

The idea. The authors build a certificate. Given any candidate approximation of the price function, they manufacture two companion numbers: a guaranteed lower bound and a guaranteed upper bound, both computed from the candidate itself. The true worst-case price is proven to sit between them. It's like a contractor who not only gives you an estimate but also a signed promise: "the real cost is at least X and at most Y."

The clever part is how the bounds are built. The lower bound comes from acting on the candidate — treating its curvature as a trading strategy and seeing what that strategy actually earns (it can only do as well or worse than optimal, hence a lower bound). The upper bound comes from a "dual" view that penalizes how badly the candidate fails to satisfy the governing equation; a perfect candidate gets zero penalty.

What they found. The two bounds genuinely sandwich the price, and crucially, the tightness of the sandwich depends on how accurately the candidate captures second-order information — the Hessian, i.e. curvature. Approximations that nail the price level but get the curvature wrong produce loose, nearly useless bounds. This is a concrete warning for the modern wave of neural-network pricers.

The Math

The setup. The UVM price $u(t,x)$ of a European option solves the Black–Scholes–Barenblatt (BSB) equation, a nonlinear PDE $$\partial_t u + \sup_{\sigma \in [\underline{\sigma},\,\overline{\sigma}]} \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 x^2\, \partial_{xx} u \;=\; 0, \qquad u(T,x) = g(x),$$ where $t$ is time, $x$ the asset price, $[\underline{\sigma},\overline{\sigma}]$ the allowed volatility band, $\partial_{xx}u$ the second derivative (the option's gamma), $g$ the payoff, and $T$ maturity. The supremum picks the worst-case volatility pointwise: high volatility where gamma is positive, low where it's negative.

The key equations. Take any smooth candidate $\hat u$. The primal (lower) bound uses the feedback control $\sigma^*(t,x) = \arg\max_\sigma \sigma^2 x^2 \partial_{xx}\hat u$ induced by the candidate's Hessian; simulating the controlled process gives a value that under-estimates the true one. The dual (upper) bound is built from a matrix-valued Gamma field $\Gamma$ and a nonnegative Hamiltonian penalty, and when $\Gamma$ is generated by the candidate itself the upper bound reduces to an expectation of the BSB residual $$\mathcal{R}[\hat u] \;=\; \partial_t \hat u + \sup_{\sigma}\tfrac12 \sigma^2 x^2 \partial_{xx}\hat u,$$ i.e. exactly how far $\hat u$ is from solving the PDE. True price $u$ then obeys $\text{(primal)} \le u \le \text{(dual)}$.

The method. The lower bound is a stochastic-control / policy evaluation step; the upper bound is a martingale duality argument (the nonnegative penalty certifies a supersolution). Both continuous quantities are then discretized in time, and the paper quantifies the extra discretization error so the final computable bounds remain valid. Candidates are produced by stochastic policy-gradient and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), and compared against a classical martingale dual benchmark.

Results with numbers. The paper is a methods/analysis contribution, so the "numbers" are qualitative but sharp: the experiments show the bound gap is dominated by the accuracy of second-order derivatives of the candidate — PINNs that match price but not gamma yield substantially looser bounds than methods that capture curvature, and the residual-based upper bound tracks the true error faithfully once discretization error is controlled.

Jargon Decoded

  • Uncertain Volatility Model (UVM) — pricing an option against the worst case over a whole interval of possible volatilities instead of one fixed value.
  • Black–Scholes–Barenblatt equation — the nonlinear PDE whose solution is the UVM worst-case price.
  • Primal–dual bounds — a matched pair of estimates, one below and one above, that provably trap the true answer.
  • Hessian / gamma — the second derivative of the option value in the asset price; measures curvature of the payoff.
  • Physics-informed neural network (PINN) — a neural net trained to satisfy a differential equation by penalizing the equation's residual during training.
  • A posteriori bound — an error estimate computed after you have a candidate solution, using the candidate itself.

Honest Take

The bounds are only as good as the candidate's derivatives, so for noisy or low-regularity approximations the sandwich can be too wide to be decision-useful — the paper is candid that curvature accuracy is the bottleneck. It's confined to European payoffs and the one-dimensional UVM here; multi-asset or path-dependent extensions aren't demonstrated. Still, as a reusable certificate bolted onto any approximate pricer, it's exactly the kind of safety rail the neural-pricing boom has been missing.

Bottom Line

Any approximate option price can now come with a proof of how far off it might be — provided your approximation gets the curvature right.

3. Buying at Support: The Entry Problem under Multi-Skew Brownian Motion

Jun Maeda
q-fin.MF · 2026-10-04 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

If a stock tends to bounce off a "support" price, the mathematically optimal moment to buy is exactly at that support level — and this paper proves it.

Plain English

The problem. Traders talk constantly about "support" and "resistance" — price floors a stock keeps bouncing up from, and ceilings it keeps bumping down against. It's folklore: buy near support, sell near resistance. But folklore isn't a theorem. If you actually believe a price gets nudged upward whenever it dips to support, when precisely should you buy, given that you'll later sell at the best possible moment? Buy too early and you tie up cash; buy too late and you miss the bounce. Everyday version: you want a house on a street where prices always seem to rebound from a floor — do you pounce the instant it hits the floor, or wait?

The idea. The authors model the price with a special kind of random walk that is "sticky" and "kinked" at the support and resistance levels — a multi-skew Brownian motion. Think of ordinary random price wiggles, but with invisible springs installed at the support and resistance prices: whenever the price lands on one of those levels, it gets statistically shoved in a preferred direction (up at support, down at resistance). The buyer's reward for entering is whatever they can later earn by selling optimally — a quantity worked out in a companion paper. So the entry decision is layered on top of an already-solved exit decision.

The beautiful result is that this two-stage problem collapses to something almost trivial. The reward-for-buying function has a curvature property (it "bulges the wrong way" inside the region where you'd keep holding) that forces the optimal buy decision to happen only at the handful of special spring-levels. With exactly one support and one resistance, the answer is unambiguous: buy right at the support, no matter the regime.

What they found. The optimal entry rule is explicit and matches the trader's intuition — but now it's a proven consequence of the model, with a formula for the value of the strategy, rather than a hunch.

The Math

The setup. The log-price is a multi-skew Brownian motion: a Brownian motion modified at finitely many levels $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ (the support/resistance levels) by skewness parameters $\beta_i \in [-1,1]$ that bias the direction the process leaves each level. The traded price is $S_t = e^{X_t}$, a geometric multi-skew Brownian motion, where $X_t$ is the skew process. Discounting uses rate $r>0$.

The key equations. The entry problem is an optimal stopping problem $$V(x) \;=\; \sup_{\tau}\; \mathbb{E}_x\!\left[e^{-r\tau}\, G(S_\tau)\right],$$ where $\tau$ ranges over buy-times (stopping times), $G$ is the exit premium — the value of the subsequently-optimal liquidation, solved in the companion paper — and $\mathbb{E}_x$ is expectation starting from log-price $x$. The decisive structural fact proved is that $G$ is strictly $r$-subharmonic inside the exit continuation region, i.e. $$\tfrac12 G'' + \mu G' - r G \;>\; 0 \quad \text{there},$$ with $G''$, $G'$ the second and first derivatives and $\mu$ the drift. Subharmonicity means the discounted reward is a strict submartingale while you wait, so it is never optimal to stop in the interior — stopping is only optimal at the skew levels where the local push ("local time") injects extra value. The value function is then the upper concave hull of $G$ evaluated at finitely many candidate points.

The method. The engine is the classic optimal-stopping-via-concave-majorant technique: after a scale transform, the value function is the smallest concave function dominating the reward, and skew points are where the reward can touch that hull. Because subharmonicity rules out the continuum of interior points, the infinite-dimensional stopping problem reduces to a finite optimization over the skew levels — and with one support + one resistance, to a single explicit comparison. The upward push at a skew level is formalized through the process's local time there.

Results with numbers. This is an exact-solution paper, so the "result" is a closed form rather than a benchmark beat: with one support and one resistance level, the optimal rule is to buy exactly at the support in every exit regime, and the value $V$ is given in explicit closed form as the concave hull of the premium at the two skew levels.

Jargon Decoded

  • Skew Brownian motion — a Brownian motion that, each time it hits a chosen level, is pushed up or down with a fixed bias instead of symmetrically.
  • Support / resistance — price levels a market tends to bounce up from (support) or fall back from (resistance).
  • Optimal stopping — the problem of choosing the best random moment to take an action to maximize expected (discounted) reward.
  • $r$-subharmonic — a function whose discounted version tends to grow if you wait, signalling "don't stop yet."
  • Concave hull / majorant — the smallest concave curve lying above a given function; in optimal stopping it is the value function.
  • Local time — a measure of how much time (and push) a process accumulates exactly at a special level.

Honest Take

The result is only as real as the model's premise — that prices are genuinely nudged upward at a known, fixed support level; in live markets support is fuzzy, shifts, and sometimes breaks entirely, so a mis-placed level makes the "optimal" rule mis-fire. It's also a single-asset, known-parameter idealization with no transaction costs or estimation error. As a clean theoretical vindication of trader folklore it's elegant; as a trading system it needs the support levels to actually exist and be estimable.

Bottom Line

Under a model where support really holds, the optimal buyer waits for the floor and pounces exactly on it — folklore, finally proven.

4. Optimal Catastrophe Bond Design and its Applications to Climate Change Risk

Andres Medina, Wei Wei
q-fin.PR · 2026-09-30 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

Instead of accepting the off-the-shelf payout schedule of a disaster bond, this paper computes the best possible schedule — and it turns out to be a simple two-line shape.

Plain English

The problem. When a hurricane flattens a city, someone has to pay. Insurers don't want to hold all that risk, so they issue catastrophe bonds ("cat bonds"): investors buy them for a juicy yield, and if a qualifying disaster strikes, the investors forfeit some or all of their money to cover losses. Everybody wins in calm years (investors earn the coupon) and the issuer is protected in bad ones. The trouble is that the payout rule — how much the bond pays per dollar of disaster loss — is usually just picked by convention: often an all-or-nothing trigger ("if losses exceed USD 1 billion, investors lose everything"). Nobody optimizes it. And all-or-nothing triggers invite moral hazard and cliff effects: a loss USD 1 just over the line behaves wildly differently from one USD 1 under.

The idea. The authors flip the design around. They treat the entire payout function — the whole curve mapping disaster size to payment — as the thing to choose, then ask: what shape minimizes the sponsor's risk for a given cost? The surprising answer is that across a broad family of sensible risk preferences, the optimal curve is always piecewise linear: pay nothing until losses cross an attachment point, then pay in direct proportion above it. No cliffs, no exotic curves — just a kink. Because the whole infinite menu of possible curves collapses to this one shape, designing the bond reduces to choosing a single number: where to put the kink.

What they found. They prove this piecewise-linear optimality under two different risk frameworks, derive explicit (or near-explicit) formulas for the best attachment point under six risk criteria, and — using real U.S. storm-loss data — show these optimized bonds cut risk substantially versus conventional designs, while the smooth kink kills the moral-hazard problem that discontinuous triggers create.

The Math

The setup. Let $X \ge 0$ be the random catastrophe loss with known distribution (calibrated from data). The bond pays the sponsor an indemnity $I(X)$, where $0 \le I(x) \le x$ is the decision variable — an entire function, making this an infinite-dimensional optimization. The sponsor's residual risk after transfer is on the retained loss $X - I(X)$ plus the cost of the bond.

The key equations. The design problem is $$\min_{0\le I(\cdot)\le \mathrm{id}} \; \rho\big(X - I(X)\big) \quad \text{subject to a cost/budget constraint on } I,$$ where $\rho$ is a risk functional — either an expected disutility $\mathbb{E}[v(X-I(X))]$ with $v$ convex, or a spectral risk measure $$\rho_\phi(L) = \int_0^1 \phi(p)\, F_L^{-1}(p)\, dp,$$ with $L$ the retained loss, $F_L^{-1}$ its quantile function, and $\phi$ a nonincreasing weight emphasizing bad outcomes. The main theorem shows the optimizer is $$I^*(x) = (x - a)^+ = \max(x - a,\, 0),$$ a piecewise linear indemnity with a single free parameter, the attachment point $a \ge 0$. The infinite-dimensional problem thereby reduces to a one-dimensional optimization over $a$, for which the paper derives explicit/semi-explicit first-order conditions, plus bounds and comparative statics (how $a^*$ moves as loss severity, cost, or risk aversion change).

The method. The optimality proof is a calculus-of-variations / pointwise-maximization argument: because both objective and constraint act through $X - I(X)$, the optimal payout can be found loss-level by loss-level, and convexity/comonotonicity arguments force the $(x-a)^+$ form (the same logic behind classic optimal-reinsurance "stop-loss" results). Calibration uses NOAA's Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters severe-storm losses to fit the loss distribution, after which $a^*$ is computed numerically under each of the six risk criteria.

Results with numbers. On the calibrated storm data, the optimized piecewise-linear cat bonds deliver substantial risk reductions across most of the six risk criteria relative to conventional (notably discontinuous-trigger) designs, and by construction the continuous $(x-a)^+$ payout removes the moral hazard associated with threshold jumps. (The paper reports the optimal attachment points and comparative statics per criterion; magnitudes vary by risk measure.)

Jargon Decoded

  • Catastrophe bond — a security whose investors lose principal to the issuer if a specified disaster occurs, transferring catastrophe risk to markets.
  • Indemnity function — the rule mapping the size of a loss to the amount the bond pays out.
  • Attachment point — the loss threshold above which the bond starts paying.
  • Spectral risk measure — a way of scoring risk that puts extra weight on the worst outcomes via a chosen weighting function.
  • Moral hazard — the distortion where a payout structure changes behavior or invites gaming, here caused by sharp payout cliffs.
  • Comparative statics — how the optimal choice shifts when an input (severity, cost, risk aversion) changes.

Honest Take

The clean $(x-a)^+$ answer leans on the loss distribution being known and well-calibrated — but catastrophe tails are exactly where data is thin and climate change is shifting the distribution out from under you, so the "optimal" attachment point inherits that estimation risk. Real cat bonds also use parametric or industry-index triggers (chosen partly to reduce verification cost and basis risk), which this indemnity-based framing abstracts away. Still, it gives issuers a principled default shape and a single, interpretable dial to set.

Bottom Line

The best disaster bond isn't a clever curve — it's a plain straight line that kicks in at one well-chosen threshold.

5. Finite-Horizon Reversible Investment under Multi-Factor Dynamics

Junkee Jeon, Takwon Kim, Jinwan Park, A. Max Reppen
q-fin.MF · 2026-09-29 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

A firm that can both build and sell off capacity should treat its expansion and contraction decisions as two separate moving walls — and this paper maps exactly where those walls sit through time.

Plain English

The problem. Imagine a company — a power producer, a data-center operator, a shipping firm — deciding how much productive capacity to own. Demand is uncertain and drifts around. Adding capacity costs money; selling it back recovers only a fraction (you buy a machine at full price but offload it at a discount). And there's a deadline: a finite planning horizon. The question is dynamic and two-sided: when do I invest more, when do I pull back, and when do I just sit tight? This is the real-options problem at the heart of corporate finance — the value isn't just in owning capacity, it's in the flexibility to adjust it as the world changes.

The idea. The authors show the whole decision organizes itself into three zones in terms of a single quantity, the marginal value of one more unit of capacity. If that marginal value is high enough, you're in the "invest" zone and should buy up to a boundary. If it's low enough, you're in the "disinvest" zone and should sell down to another boundary. In between is a "wait" zone where you touch nothing. Because selling recovers less than buying costs, there's a gap between the buy-wall and the sell-wall — a band of inaction, exactly the hysteresis real managers show. The twist versus textbook treatments is that demand here is driven by several correlated random factors, not one, and the horizon is finite, so the walls move as the deadline approaches.

What they found. They rigorously establish that this three-zone structure is correct (the boundaries exist, are continuous, and never collide), and they compute how the walls respond to the economy. The striking numerical finding: improvements in demand shift the two walls by different amounts — the disinvestment wall is much more sensitive — and the effects compound super-additively when multiple demand factors improve together.

The Math

The setup. Demand/profitability is driven by a multi-factor geometric Brownian motion $Y_t = (Y_t^1,\dots,Y_t^d)$ with correlations across factors. A risk-neutral firm controls its capacity $C_t$, which it can increase (pay proportional cost $p$ per unit) or decrease (recover salvage $s < p$ per unit). The control is singular — capacity is a monotone-adjustable process, not a rate. The firm maximizes expected discounted profit over $[0,T]$.

The key equations. By the singular-control ↔ optimal-switching correspondence, the right object is the marginal value of capacity $u = \partial_C V$, where $V$ is the firm's value function. This $u$ solves a family of parabolic double-obstacle problems $$\min\Big\{\, \max\big\{\, -\partial_t u - \mathcal{L}u - \pi,\; u - p \,\big\},\; s - u \,\Big\} = 0,$$ where $\mathcal{L}$ is the generator of the multi-factor diffusion $Y$, $\pi$ is the marginal profit flow, $p$ the purchase cost, $s$ the salvage value, and the two obstacles $u \le p$ and $u \ge s$ encode "don't invest past where a unit is worth its cost" and "don't disinvest past where it's worth its salvage." The state space splits into three regions: $$\{u = p\}\ \text{(invest)}, \qquad \{s < u < p\}\ \text{(wait)}, \qquad \{u = s\}\ \text{(disinvest)},$$ separated by free boundaries that move with time $t$ and factor levels $Y$.

The method. The authors prove existence, uniqueness, and local Sobolev ($W^{2,1}_{p,\text{loc}}$) regularity of a strong solution to the double-obstacle PDE, then show the free boundaries are continuous and strictly separated (the invest and disinvest walls never touch, guaranteeing a genuine wait region). A verification theorem confirms the candidate is the true value function and that the optimal capacity is the associated reflected process — capacity is nudged just enough to stay inside the wait band, like a ball kept between two moving walls. Solutions are computed numerically.

Results with numbers. Numerically, a joint improvement in demand factors shifts both free boundaries super-additively — the combined effect exceeds the sum of individual effects — and the shift is 1.5 to 2.7 times stronger at the disinvestment boundary than at the investment boundary, with the exact multiple depending on the correlation between factors. In plain terms: good news makes firms much more reluctant to shrink than eager to grow, and correlated good news amplifies that asymmetry.

Jargon Decoded

  • Real option — the value of managerial flexibility to invest, wait, or divest as conditions change, treated like a financial option.
  • Singular control — a control that adjusts a cumulative quantity (like total capacity) in possibly abrupt, monotone moves rather than at a bounded rate.
  • Double-obstacle problem — a PDE whose solution is squeezed between an upper and a lower barrier, each barrier active in a different region.
  • Free boundary — the (unknown, to-be-solved-for) curve separating "act" regions from the "wait" region.
  • Reflected process — a process kept inside a region by minimal pushes at its edges, the mathematical image of "adjust only as much as needed."
  • Geometric Brownian motion — the standard model for a positive quantity that grows with random proportional shocks.

Honest Take

The firm is risk-neutral with proportional (linear) adjustment costs and geometric-Brownian demand — convenient assumptions that real capital projects violate through fixed/lumpy costs, construction delays, and fat-tailed shocks, any of which can reshape the boundaries. It's also a single-firm problem with no competitive reaction. But the qualitative payoff — a rigorously justified invest/wait/disinvest band and a quantified asymmetry in how the two walls respond — is genuinely useful intuition for anyone modeling capacity under uncertainty.

Bottom Line

Flexible firms run on two moving walls with a do-nothing gap between them, and good news pushes the "sell" wall far harder than the "build" wall.
