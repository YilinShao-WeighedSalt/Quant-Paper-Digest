1. A Rough Instantaneous Squared VIX Model for Term Structure and Option Pricing

Tianfeng Yang, Wen Su, Blanka Horvath
q-fin.PR · 2026-11-04 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

Giving a volatility model a memory of its own recent past — so today's turbulence still carries the echo of last week's — makes options on the "fear gauge" price measurably better, especially when markets are rough.

Plain English

The problem. The VIX is Wall Street's "fear gauge": a number, published every second, that estimates how much the US stock market expects to swing over the next 30 days. When investors are calm it sits near 12–15; in a panic it spikes above 40. Because the VIX itself jumps around, traders buy and sell options on the VIX — bets that pay off if fear rises above some level — to protect portfolios against turbulence. To price those bets fairly you need a mathematical model of how the VIX will move. Get the model wrong and you either overpay for insurance or sell it too cheap.

The idea. Almost every standard model assumes volatility is memoryless: where it goes tomorrow depends only on where it is today, not on the path it took to get here. That is convenient but false. Real volatility behaves more like a struck bell — a shock doesn't vanish the next instant, it rings and decays slowly, and the market "remembers" it for days. This paper builds that memory in directly. Instead of letting the squared VIX follow the usual memoryless recipe, they drive it with a rough process whose influence fades according to a slow, stretched-out decay curve (a "Mittag–Leffler" curve — think of an exponential that has been pulled flatter so its tail lingers). The cost of memory is usually that the math becomes intractable; their trick is to keep the model in a special "affine" family where a single master function — the characteristic function — can still be written down, so option prices follow from a Fourier transform rather than brute-force simulation.

What they found. Across daily VIX term-structure data and VIX option quotes from January 2018 to June 2025, the memory-equipped model (RISVIX) beat the memoryless benchmark at fitting the term structure (how expected volatility differs across horizons), with the biggest wins during stressed, high-VIX periods and on the shape — the slope and curvature — of that curve. Out-of-sample it also priced options better, cutting mean-squared error by about 13% and mean absolute error by about 6%, with the largest gains at short maturities and in exactly the turbulent regimes where mispricing hurts most.

The Math

The setup. Let $V_t = \mathrm{VIX}_t^2$ be the instantaneous squared VIX. In the classical Markovian (memoryless) model $V_t$ is a mean-reverting diffusion, $$dV_t = \kappa(\theta - V_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{V_t}\,dW_t,$$ where $\kappa>0$ is the speed of mean reversion, $\theta>0$ the long-run level it is pulled toward, $\sigma$ the volatility-of-volatility, and $W_t$ a Brownian motion. "Memoryless" shows up because the increment $dV_t$ depends only on the current value $V_t$.

The RISVIX model replaces this with a stochastic Volterra equation in which past increments are reweighted by a power-law kernel: $$V_t = V_0 + \int_0^t K(t-s)\,\kappa(\theta - V_s)\,ds + \int_0^t K(t-s)\,\sigma\sqrt{V_s}\,dW_s,\qquad K(u)=\frac{u^{H-1/2}}{\Gamma(H+1/2)},$$ where $K$ is the fractional kernel, $H\in(0,1/2)$ is the roughness (Hurst) parameter — smaller $H$ means rougher, longer-remembered paths — and $\Gamma$ is the gamma function. The ordinary model is the boundary case $H=1/2$, where $K$ collapses to a constant and memory disappears.

The key equation. Because the drift is affine in $V$, the conditional mean of future squared VIX — the term structure — admits an explicit representation governed by a Mittag–Leffler function: $$\mathbb{E}[\,V_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t\,] = \theta + (V_t-\theta)\,E_{\alpha}\!\big(-\kappa (T-t)^{\alpha}\big),$$ where $E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{n\ge 0} z^n/\Gamma(\alpha n+1)$ is the Mittag–Leffler function, $\alpha$ is tied to the memory exponent, $T-t$ is the horizon, and $\mathcal{F}_t$ is the information available at time $t$. For $\alpha=1$ this reduces to ordinary exponential decay $e^{-\kappa(T-t)}$; for $\alpha<1$ the decay is slower, bending the term structure the way real data demand.

The method. The model stays affine in the sense that gives a semi-closed-form characteristic/Fourier–Laplace transform of $\int V$, so VIX-option prices are obtained by numerical Fourier inversion rather than Monte Carlo. Calibration is two-step: (1) fit the memory parameter, mean reversion, and date-specific initial states to the VIX term-structure indices; (2) fit the remaining distributional parameters to observed VIX option prices.

Results with numbers. On 2018–2025 data, out-of-sample option-pricing error fell 13.06% (MSE) and 6.33% (MAE) versus the Markovian benchmark, concentrated at short maturities and in high-VIX states; term-structure fit improved most on slope and curvature.

Jargon Decoded

  • VIX — a published index estimating the stock market's expected 30-day volatility, the "fear gauge."
  • Term structure — how the expected quantity (here volatility) varies with the time horizon you look at.
  • Rough / fractional volatility — a volatility process with long memory and very jagged paths, encoded by a Hurst parameter $H<1/2$.
  • Mittag–Leffler function — a generalization of the exponential whose decay can be slower-than-exponential, natural for fractional (memory) dynamics.
  • Affine model — a model whose drift and variance are linear in the state, which makes the characteristic function solvable and Fourier pricing possible.
  • Fourier–Laplace transform pricing — recovering option prices by inverting a known transform of the payoff distribution instead of simulating.

Honest Take

The gains, while real, are modest (single-digit to low-double-digit percentage error reductions) and come with extra parameters and a heavier calibration pipeline, so a desk must judge whether the improvement justifies the complexity. Fractional models are also notoriously finicky to estimate stably, and the "memory" story is strongest precisely in rare high-volatility episodes where data are thin. It is a credible, tractable addition to the rough-volatility toolkit rather than a revolution.

Bottom Line

Volatility has a memory, and modeling that memory — without giving up closed-form pricing — pays off most exactly when markets are scariest.

2. Neural Realized Measures: Forecast-Supervised Intraday Aggregation for Volatility Forecasting

Brian Sing Fan Chan, Jennifer So Kuen Chan
q-fin.ST · 2026-11-05 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

Instead of mushing a day's worth of price ticks into a volatility number with a fixed recipe and then forecasting, let the forecasting goal itself decide how to weight those ticks — and you forecast better.

Plain English

The problem. Traders and risk managers desperately want to know how much an asset will swing tomorrow — that's "volatility forecasting." Modern markets record thousands of price ticks per day, so the standard pipeline has two stages. First, squeeze all of today's intraday ticks into a single daily number called a "realized measure" (roughly, add up the squared tiny price moves). Second, feed that one number into a forecasting model. The catch: stage one uses a fixed rule that treats every moment of the day as equally informative, decided before anyone thinks about forecasting. A frantic minute around a Fed announcement and a sleepy lunchtime minute get summed the same way, even though they may matter very differently for tomorrow's risk.

The idea. Why not let the forecasting objective reach back and reshape how the day is summarized? The authors propose Neural Realized Measures (NRM): keep the exact same intraday building blocks as the classic measure, but replace the "weight everything equally" step with learned, context-dependent weights — a small neural network decides how much each piece of the day should count, given what's happening. Crucially they constrain the weights to stay positive and to average to one, so the learned measure is still a genuine weighted average of the same primitives — not some opaque black box. If the network learns to make all weights equal, you get the old measure back exactly; the classic construction is literally a special case. And because it's still an explicit weighted sum, you can point at any single minute and say how much it contributed — you keep interpretability.

What they found. On simulated data (a Heston volatility model, where the "true" answer is known) NRM reduced forecast error for both same-day and next-day integrated variance across every combination of building-block and market regime they tried. On six real cryptocurrencies — a brutal, noisy, around-the-clock test bed — it kept the edge. The gain comes not from a new exotic statistic but from letting the downstream goal supervise the summary step that was previously frozen.

The Math

The setup. Over trading day $d$, split time into $n$ intraday bars indexed $i=1,\dots,n$. A classical raw realized measure (RRM) is a sum of per-bar primitive contributions $c_{d,i}$ (for realized variance, $c_{d,i}=r_{d,i}^2$, the squared return of bar $i$): $$\mathrm{RRM}_d=\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{d,i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\cdot n\,c_{d,i},$$ i.e. an implicitly uniform aggregation. The target to forecast is the latent integrated variance $\mathrm{IV}_d=\int_{d}\sigma_t^2\,dt$, where $\sigma_t$ is the (unobserved) instantaneous volatility.

The key equation. NRM replaces uniform weights with positive, mean-normalized, context-dependent weights $w_{d,i}$: $$\mathrm{NRM}_d=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_{d,i}\,c_{d,i},\qquad w_{d,i}=\frac{\exp\!\big(g_\phi(x_{d,i})\big)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(g_\phi(x_{d,j})\big)}\;>0,\qquad \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w_{d,i}=1,$$ where $g_\phi$ is a neural network with parameters $\phi$, $x_{d,i}$ is a feature vector describing the context of bar $i$ (e.g. time of day, recent activity), and the normalization forces the weights to average to one so scale is preserved. Setting $g_\phi\equiv \text{const}$ gives $w_{d,i}=1$ and recovers $\mathrm{RRM}_d$ exactly — the classical measure is nested.

The method. The weights are learned end-to-end by minimizing a downstream forecasting loss: plug $\mathrm{NRM}_d$ into a forecasting specification (SF, HAR, or MIDAS — standard autoregressive volatility models) and train $\phi$ so next-period forecasts are accurate. This is "forecast-supervised aggregation": the measurement stage is optimized for the prediction it feeds, not in isolation. Because $\mathrm{NRM}_d$ stays an explicit weighted sum of the original $c_{d,i}$, bar-level attribution survives.

Results with numbers. In Heston simulations, NRM reduced RMSE for both contemporaneous and next-day latent integrated variance across all evaluated primitive–regime combinations relative to the matching RRM. The improvement carried over to six real cryptocurrency markets under controlled comparisons against HAR and MIDAS baselines.

Jargon Decoded

  • Realized measure / realized variance — a volatility estimate built by summing squared high-frequency returns over a day.
  • Integrated variance — the true, unobservable total variance accumulated over a day, the thing forecasts aim at.
  • HAR / MIDAS — standard regression models that predict volatility from past realized measures at mixed horizons/frequencies.
  • Heston model — a benchmark stochastic-volatility model used in simulation because its "true" integrated variance is known.
  • Primitive-preserving — the learned measure reuses the exact same per-bar quantities as the classical one, only reweighting them.
  • Mean-normalized weights — weights forced to average to one, so reweighting changes emphasis but not overall scale.

Honest Take

The architecture is deliberately conservative — a reweighting layer, not a free-form predictor — which is both its virtue (interpretable, nested, hard to overfit wildly) and its ceiling (it can't capture structure outside a weighted sum of existing primitives). The crypto evidence is encouraging but crypto is unusually volatile and may flatter a method that shines in turbulent regimes; equities and FX would be a sterner test. Still, the idea of supervising the measurement stage by the forecast is clean and portable.

Bottom Line

Stop summarizing your data in the dark — let the question you actually care about decide what counts.

3. Necessary and sufficient conditions for the martingale property of stochastic exponentials driven by multi-dimensional diffusions

Erhan Bayraktar, Hao Xing
math.PR · 2026-11-04 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

This paper gives an exact yes/no test for whether a certain formula built from a random process is a "fair bet" that neither drifts up nor leaks probability — a technical condition that quietly decides whether your option-pricing math is even valid.

Plain English

The problem. A huge amount of finance rests on a trick: to price an option, you mathematically "re-tilt" the probabilities so that every asset, on average, just grows at the risk-free rate — a so-called risk-neutral world. That re-tilting is done by multiplying by a special gadget called a stochastic exponential. The whole method only works if that gadget is a true martingale — loosely, a perfectly fair running bet whose expected future value always equals its present value, with no hidden drift and no probability quietly escaping to infinity. Here's the uncomfortable secret: in many realistic models the gadget looks like a martingale but is secretly only a "local" one that leaks — and when it leaks, your change of probabilities is broken, prices come out wrong, and "bubbles" appear in the math. So practitioners badly need a clean test: for this model, with these coefficients, is the gadget a genuine martingale or not?

The idea. The authors deliver necessary-and-sufficient conditions — a true if-and-only-if test — for multi-dimensional diffusion models, not just toy one-dimensional ones. Their strategy: translate the martingale question into a completely different one about geometry and escape. A classical result says the gadget is a true martingale exactly when a related process, running under the re-tilted probabilities, never escapes its allowed region (never "explodes" to the boundary or to infinity in finite time). So they carve the boundary of the model's domain into pieces — smooth walls, sharp corners, and the point at infinity — and give a precise test for each type of piece: at smooth walls, a sign condition on a drift; at infinity, a one-dimensional "Feller test" for explosion; and extra tests for the awkward in-between cases. Stitch the pieces together and you get the full answer.

What they found. They produce explicit, checkable criteria for workhorse models — the inverse-CIR and the "4/2" stochastic-volatility models with correlated factors — including the delicate borderline cases where previous affine/Riccati shortcuts simply fail to decide. They also show these same conditions double as uniqueness criteria for the pricing equations themselves.

The Math

The setup. Work on a filtered probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_t),\mathbb{P})$ carrying an $n$-dimensional diffusion $X_t$ solving $$dX_t=b(X_t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t)\,dW_t,\qquad X_t\in D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n,$$ with drift $b$, diffusion matrix $\sigma$, $W_t$ an $n$-dimensional Brownian motion, and $D$ the state domain. Given an integrand $\theta(X_t)$ (the "market price of risk" vector), form the stochastic exponential $$Z_t=\exp\!\Big(\int_0^t \theta(X_s)^\top dW_s-\tfrac12\int_0^t \lVert\theta(X_s)\rVert^2\,ds\Big).$$ $Z_t$ is always a nonnegative local martingale and hence a supermartingale, so $\mathbb{E}[Z_t]\le 1$; it is a true martingale iff $\mathbb{E}[Z_t]=1$ for all $t$, which is exactly what legitimizes using $Z_t$ to define a new measure $d\mathbb{Q}=Z_t\,d\mathbb{P}$.

The key equations. By Girsanov's theorem, under the candidate measure $\mathbb{Q}$ the diffusion acquires a shifted drift $\tilde b=b+\sigma\sigma^\top\theta$, and the martingale property becomes equivalent to non-explosion of the $\mathbb{Q}$-diffusion $\tilde X$: $$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[Z_t]=1\ \ \forall t\iff \mathbb{P}(\tilde\tau_{\mathrm{explode}}=\infty)=1,$$ where $\tilde\tau_{\mathrm{explode}}$ is the first time $\tilde X$ hits the boundary of $D$ or runs off to infinity. On a smooth boundary point, non-exit is governed by the sign of the shifted Fichera drift $f(x)$ (the inward/outward pull of $\tilde b$ at the wall); the sharp criterion is $f(x)\le 0$, plus an explicit local condition where $f$ vanishes and the integrand $\theta$ is unbounded. At infinity, when a scalar functional of $X$ reduces to a one-dimensional diffusion up to a bounded time change, Feller's test for explosions gives exact criteria via the scale and speed measures.

The method. Decompose $\partial D$ into (i) smooth boundary points, (ii) infinity, and (iii) nonsmooth pieces; apply the Fichera-drift sign test, Feller's test, and radial/projection tests respectively; combine. Applications: inverse-CIR and correlated 4/2 models with singular integrands, settling critical equality cases that affine Riccati methods cannot, plus examples with logarithmic boundary singularities and absorbed factors.

Results. Fully explicit necessary-and-sufficient conditions for the named models, including borderline equality cases and configurations with boundary exits undetectable by tests at infinity alone; the criteria additionally yield uniqueness for the associated valuation PDEs.

Jargon Decoded

  • Martingale — a process whose expected future value, given today, equals today's value: a fair bet with no drift.
  • Local vs. true martingale — a local martingale can secretly leak expected value (dropping below 1 here); only a true martingale legitimizes the change of measure.
  • Stochastic exponential — the multiplicative factor $Z_t$ used to re-weight probabilities (e.g. into the risk-neutral measure).
  • Girsanov's theorem — the rule for how a diffusion's drift changes when you re-tilt probabilities by $Z_t$.
  • Feller's test — a classical criterion deciding whether a one-dimensional diffusion can explode (reach a boundary) in finite time.
  • Fichera drift — the boundary drift term whose sign governs whether a diffusion can exit through a smooth boundary.

Honest Take

This is rigorous probability, not a trading strategy: its value is defensive — it tells a modeler whether their pricing framework is mathematically sound before they trust prices or hedges out of it. The conditions, while sharp, require real analytic work to verify for a given model (classifying boundary behavior is not push-button), so it's a reference for quants building new volatility models rather than something a desk runs daily. Its scope is diffusions; jumps are out.

Bottom Line

Before you re-tilt the odds to price an option, this paper tells you exactly whether the re-tilting is legal — and in the models where it most often isn't.

4. Non-Convex Market Makers: Coherence without Convexity, Friction, and Parallel Composition

Peter Cotton
q-fin.MF · 2026-10-31 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

The automated "house" that quotes prices in crypto and prediction markets doesn't actually need the sacred mathematical property everyone assumed (convexity) to be arbitrage-free — it needs a simpler condition, and a small fee fixes the rest.

Plain English

The problem. Many modern markets have no human market maker; instead an automated market maker (AMM) — a formula — quotes a price for anything you want to buy or sell. You see these in prediction markets ("will candidate X win?") and in crypto exchanges like Uniswap. The formula is built from a cost function: the market tracks a running cost, and the price of a contract is how much that cost rises when you buy one more unit. For twenty years the field has assumed this cost function must be convex (curving upward like a bowl). Convexity was treated as the thing that keeps the market "coherent" — meaning you can't construct a riskless money pump (an arbitrage) against it. But is convexity really the source of that safety, or just a convenient companion assumption that got conflated with it?

The idea. The authors separate the two. They show the exact condition for no-arbitrage is not convexity but a chord condition: if you draw any straight line (a chord) between two points on the cost curve, its slope must lie within the range of possible payoffs. That's it. Convexity sneaks into the usual textbook axioms only through a different, separate requirement — that the maker always incorporate new information monotonically. Drop that extra requirement and you're left with perfectly coherent market makers that are not convex. There's a price for giving up convexity: a rational trader will always trade against the "convex envelope" (the taut rubber band stretched under your curve), so the wiggly non-convex parts are simply never used — non-convexity costs you expressiveness, not safety. And if you want to rule out even the harmless arbitrage, a proportional fee at least as big as the deepest arbitrage opportunity restores strict no-arbitrage — which, viewed another way, is exactly a bid–ask spread with a "no-trade" dead zone. They even show how to combine several such fee-charging makers into one aggregate (the math of stacking order books), and that adding a smooth quadratic co-quoter is a known smoothing operation that never breaks coherence.

What they found. A clean reconceptualization: coherence = chord condition; convexity = a consequence of an orthogonal axiom you can discard; fees = friction that both restores no-arbitrage and literally is a bid–ask spread; and arbitrageurs function as a decentralized mechanism that detects and corrects any incoherent quote.

The Math

The setup. Consider a finite outcome space with payoff vectors; a path-independent market maker is defined by a cost function $C:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}$ over inventory/share vectors $q\in\mathbb{R}^k$. The instantaneous price vector is the gradient $p(q)=\nabla C(q)$, and the cost of a trade moving inventory from $q$ to $q'$ is $C(q')-C(q)$. Payoffs live in a set $\Pi$; the market pays $\langle q,\pi\rangle$ in outcome $\pi\in\Pi$.

The key condition. No-arbitrage is equivalent to the chord condition: for all $q,q'$, $$\frac{C(q')-C(q)}{\lVert q'-q\rVert}\ \text{(every chord slope)}\ \in\ \mathrm{conv}(\Pi),$$ i.e. every secant slope of $C$ lies in the convex hull of the payoff vectors. Equivalently, there is no direction $q$ with $$C(q)-C(0)<\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\langle q,\pi\rangle,$$ which would mean buying bundle $q$ for less than its worst-case payoff — a guaranteed profit. Here $\mathrm{conv}(\Pi)$ is the convex hull of payoffs, $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ the inner product. Rational flow trades the convex envelope $C^{**}$ (the biconjugate, the largest convex function below $C$), so only $C^{**}$ is economically realized.

The friction result. A proportional fee of rate at least the arbitrage depth $\delta$ (the largest chord-condition violation) restores strict no-arbitrage; by convex conjugacy this fee is exactly a bid–ask spread with an explicit no-trade interval $$[\,p(q)-\delta,\ p(q)+\delta\,]$$ within which no trade occurs, for any cost function whatever.

The method. Combining fee-bearing makers is an infimal convolution, solved by a single monotone clearing-price root-find (one scalar equation), whose aggregate is a consolidated limit order book with sparse participation. A deep quadratic co-quoter acts as Moreau smoothing (Moreau–Yosida regularization), which preserves coherence at every depth. Any configuration outside the coherent hull admits a portfolio priced below its worst payoff, so arbitrageurs behave as decentralized separation oracles that certify and remove incoherence.

Results. The paper is structural rather than numerical: its "results" are exact equivalences — chord condition $\Leftrightarrow$ no-arbitrage; fee $\ge\delta$ $\Leftrightarrow$ bid–ask spread restoring soundness; infimal convolution $\Leftrightarrow$ single root-find for aggregation — established constructively.

Jargon Decoded

  • Automated market maker (AMM) — a formula that quotes buy/sell prices automatically, with no human on the other side.
  • Cost function — the running total cost a market tracks; its slope (gradient) is the current price.
  • Convex hull / convex envelope — the smallest "bowl-shaped" set or function containing the given points/curve; the taut rubber band underneath.
  • Arbitrage — a trade or portfolio that guarantees profit with no risk; markets are "coherent" when none exists.
  • Infimal convolution — the operation that combines two cost functions into the cheapest joint way to fill an order.
  • Moreau smoothing — rounding off a function's kinks by adding a quadratic term, without changing its essential shape.

Honest Take

This is foundational theory, not an implementation guide: it clarifies what makes an AMM safe and frees designers from an assumption they didn't need, but it doesn't hand you a turnkey better exchange. Real AMMs also face adverse selection, gas costs, and strategic liquidity providers that this clean friction model abstracts away, and "a fee at least the arbitrage depth" can be expensive if your chosen cost function is badly non-convex. Its main payoff is conceptual leverage for mechanism designers.

Bottom Line

Convexity was never the thing keeping automated markets honest — a chord condition and a well-sized fee are, and knowing the difference widens the design space.

5. Boom-Busty Marketron as a Model of Market Dynamics: How Investor-Market Reflexivity Amplifies Non-Equilibrium

Igor Halperin
q-fin.ST · 2026-10-31 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

If you let a big investor's buying depend on how the market has been doing — and the market depend on that buying — you get a self-feeding loop that naturally produces boom-and-bust cycles, and a toy model of exactly that loop reproduces a startling amount of real market behavior.

Plain English

The problem. Why do markets have booms and busts at all? Standard finance leans on "equilibrium": prices sit near fair value and random news nudges them around. But real markets look more like something alive — they overshoot, crash, and cycle in ways that pure randomness struggles to explain. George Soros famously argued the missing ingredient is reflexivity: investors' beliefs move prices, and those prices then warp the investors' beliefs, in a feedback loop that pushes markets far from any calm equilibrium. The problem for a quant is to turn that story into actual equations that you can simulate and test against data.

The idea. The authors build the Boom-Busty Marketron — picture a single "collective investor" (a stand-in for the crowd) whose demand pushes prices up or down. The twist: that demand itself depends on how the market has been performing. Good recent performance emboldens the crowd to buy more, which pushes prices higher, which emboldens them further — until something snaps. This closes the reflexive loop. Mathematically the market settles not into a still equilibrium but into a non-equilibrium steady state (NESS): a condition that looks statistically stable over time but has a hidden "current" endlessly circulating between mispricing, demand, and volatility — like water in a fountain, forever moving yet forever looking the same. That circulation is what makes time run forward in a detectable way (an "arrow of time") and what generates cycles. Ordinary stochastic-volatility models with a "leverage effect" already break the stillness a bit; the investor–market loop shoves the system much further from equilibrium.

What they found. Calibrated to the S&P 500 from 1996 to 2024 (by matching simulated moments), the model reproduces a remarkable checklist of real phenomena at once: the usual fat-tailed, clustered return patterns; cross-sectional facts; a boom-bust cycle whose frequency matches the S&P's within one standard deviation; default rates near those of investment-grade issuers; mispricings within Fisher Black's famous "factor of two" of fair value; the "dumb-money effect" (retail flows that predict underperformance); and a measurable arrow of time. Trained only on 1996–2010 data, its cycle statistics stay stable for a century and a half of simulation — a strong sign the dynamics are structurally stationary, not fit to a lucky window.

The Math

The setup. The state carries (at least) a mispricing variable $x_t$ (log price minus a fundamental value), the collective investor's demand/position $\phi_t$, and a stochastic variance $v_t$. Prices move with demand; demand responds to performance; variance responds to returns. Schematically the coupled dynamics are $$ \begin{aligned} dx_t &= \big(\mu(\phi_t,x_t)\big)\,dt+\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^x_t,\\ d\phi_t &= g\big(R_t,x_t\big)\,dt+\eta\,dW^\phi_t,\\ dv_t &= \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,dt+\xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^v_t, \end{aligned} $$ where $x_t$ is mispricing, $\phi_t$ the collective investor's position, $v_t$ the instantaneous variance, $R_t$ a measure of recent market performance (the reflexive input), $\mu$ the demand-driven drift, $g$ the performance-feedback function closing the loop, $\kappa,\theta,\xi$ the variance's mean-reversion speed, level and vol-of-vol, $\eta$ the demand noise scale, and $W^x,W^\phi,W^v$ correlated Brownian motions (the $x$–$v$ correlation is the leverage effect).

The key concept — broken detailed balance. A system is in true equilibrium when its stationary distribution $\rho(z)$ satisfies detailed balance: the probability current $$J(z)=\mu(z)\,\rho(z)-\tfrac12\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(D(z)\rho(z)\big)$$ vanishes everywhere, where $z=(x,\phi,v)$ is the state, $\mu$ the drift field, and $D$ the diffusion matrix. In a non-equilibrium steady state, $\rho$ is stationary ($\partial_t\rho=0$) but $J(z)\neq 0$: probability circulates in closed loops. The nonzero curl of $J$ is precisely the boom-bust cycle, and its existence gives a strictly positive entropy production rate, the mathematical signature of an arrow of time. The reflexive feedback $g(R_t,x_t)$ is what forces $J\neq 0$ beyond what leverage alone produces.

The method. Parameters are estimated by the method of simulated moments — simulate long paths, compute summary statistics (moments of returns, cycle frequency, cross-sectional spreads), and tune parameters until simulated moments match the S&P 500's, 1996–2024. Stationarity and cycle robustness are checked by running the 1996–2010-calibrated model forward over ~150 simulated years.

Results with numbers. Cycle frequency matches the S&P within one-sigma; simulated default rates are of the same order as investment-grade issuers; pricing errors stay within Black's factor-of-two band; the dumb-money and long-short factor-rotation effects emerge endogenously; and a nonzero entropy-production arrow of time is measurable in both model and data.

Jargon Decoded

  • Reflexivity — Soros's idea that beliefs move prices and prices reshape beliefs, in a self-reinforcing loop.
  • Non-equilibrium steady state (NESS) — a statistically stable state that nonetheless has persistent internal circulation, unlike a true resting equilibrium.
  • Detailed balance — the equilibrium condition that every micro-flow is exactly balanced by its reverse; its breaking signals genuine irreversibility.
  • Leverage effect — the empirical tendency for volatility to rise when prices fall (negative return–volatility correlation).
  • Method of simulated moments — estimating parameters by matching statistics of simulated data to statistics of real data.
  • Arrow of time / entropy production — a quantitative asymmetry showing the dynamics run forward differently than backward.
  • Dumb-money effect — the documented pattern that aggregate retail fund flows tend to predict lower subsequent returns.

Honest Take

This is an econophysics explanatory model, not a pricing or trading engine: its triumph is qualitative-made-quantitative — showing one reflexive loop can jointly generate many stylized facts — rather than beating a benchmark on out-of-sample prediction. The "single collective investor" is a bold simplification of a heterogeneous crowd, and matching moments is weaker evidence than forecasting; whether the identified cycle is tradable (or just descriptively real) is left open. Treat it as insight into market mechanism, not a signal.

Bottom Line

Markets may boom and bust not despite being stable but because their stability is a restless, circulating non-equilibrium — a fountain, not a still pond.
