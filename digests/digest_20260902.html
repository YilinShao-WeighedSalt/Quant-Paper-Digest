1. Single- and Multilevel Quadrature with Error Control for Fourier Pricing under the Rough Heston Model

Chiheb Ben Hammouda, Abderrahmene Ben Romdhane, Michael Samet, Raul F. Tempone
q-fin.CP · 2026-08-31 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When your volatility model is "rough," each option price hides a fiddly memory-laden equation you must solve thousands of times — this paper works out exactly how much effort each solve deserves, so you stop burning compute on precision nobody needs.

Plain English

The problem. An option is a contract that pays off depending on how far a stock swings — a call option, say, pays you the amount by which the price finishes above some agreed level. To put a fair price on it you need a model of how volatile the stock is. The classic Heston model is popular because it lets volatility itself wobble randomly instead of staying fixed. But traders noticed real market volatility is jagged — it zig-zags far more erratically than Heston allows. The fix is the rough Heston model, where volatility follows a "rough" path with a kind of long memory. It fits real option prices beautifully. The catch: pricing becomes computationally brutal. In classic Heston you get a clean formula; in rough Heston, every single point of a numerical integral requires solving a nasty fractional Riccati equation — a differential equation with memory, where the future depends on the entire past.

The idea. Think of it like cooking a big meal where some dishes need constant stirring and others you can leave alone. A naive programmer solves every one of those memory-equations with the same fine time-grid — enormous overkill for the easy points, too coarse for the hard ones. The authors instead adapt the effort. They build integration rules ("quadrature") that automatically trade off two competing errors: the error from chopping time too coarsely when solving the equation, versus the error from using too few integration points. Their cleverest move is multilevel: first compute a cheap, blurry answer everywhere, then add small correction layers only where they actually change the result — a rough charcoal sketch followed by targeted touch-ups, rather than painting every square inch in full detail.

What they found. The multilevel scheme is provably cheaper than the single-level one, and in head-to-head timing it beat the standard "BL2 Markovian approximation" (a rival shortcut) on total CPU time. So you get the same accuracy for meaningfully less computer time.

The Math

The setup. Under the rough Heston model the option price is recovered by Fourier pricing: the price is an integral of the form $$P = \int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}\!\big[\,\phi(u)\,g(u)\,\big]\,du,$$ where $\phi(u)$ is the characteristic function of the log-price (the Fourier transform of its probability law) and $g(u)$ packages the payoff and damping. In rough Heston, $\phi(u)$ has no closed form: evaluating it at each frequency $u$ requires numerically solving a fractional Riccati equation $$D^{\alpha} h(u,t) = -\tfrac12(u^2+iu) + \kappa\,\xi\,h + \tfrac12 \xi^2 h^2,$$ where $D^\alpha$ is a fractional derivative of order $\alpha=H+\tfrac12$ (with $H$ the Hurst roughness parameter), $\kappa$ is mean-reversion speed, $\xi$ the vol-of-vol, and $h$ the unknown. The fractional derivative is what encodes "memory."

The key error model. They assume the time-discretization error of the fractional solve is $O(\Delta t^{\,p})$, the cost of one characteristic-function evaluation is $O(\Delta t^{-\beta})$, and the Gauss–Laguerre quadrature error is $O(N^{-s_{SL}/2})$, where $\Delta t$ is the time step, $N$ the number of quadrature nodes, $p$ the discretization order, $\beta$ the cost exponent, and $s_{SL}$ a smoothness index. A key trick is scaling the Laguerre weight to the integrand's estimated decay so nodes land where the integrand actually lives.

The method. Gauss–Laguerre quadrature approximates the semi-infinite integral by a weighted sum at cleverly chosen nodes. The single-level method splits a target tolerance $\varepsilon$ between the two error sources. The multilevel method writes the integrand as a coarse level-zero term plus a telescoping sum of level differences, and picks quadrature nodes separately per level — cheap coarse levels do the bulk work, expensive fine levels only correct.

Results with numbers. They prove the single-level method needs $$W_{SL}=O\!\big(\varepsilon^{-(\beta/p + 2/s_{SL})}\big)$$ work for accuracy $\varepsilon$, while the multilevel method needs only $$W_{ML}=O\!\big(\varepsilon^{-\beta/p}\big),$$ dropping the $2/s_{SL}$ penalty entirely. Numerics confirm the predicted fractional-Riccati and integrand convergence rates and root-exponential quadrature behaviour, show large cuts in quadrature cost from the weight scaling, and report lower total CPU than the BL2 Markovian benchmark.

Jargon Decoded

  • Characteristic function — the Fourier transform of a random variable's distribution; knowing it is equivalent to knowing the full probability law.
  • Fractional Riccati equation — a quadratic differential equation whose derivative is "fractional," meaning the rate of change depends on the entire history, not just the present.
  • Hurst parameter $H$ — a number in $(0,1)$ measuring path roughness; $H=\tfrac12$ is ordinary Brownian motion, and rough vol uses $H$ near $0.1$.
  • Gauss–Laguerre quadrature — a rule for approximating integrals over $[0,\infty)$ by a weighted sum at optimally placed points, exact for polynomials times a decaying weight.
  • Multilevel method — a strategy that computes a cheap coarse estimate and refines it with successively finer, cheaper-to-sample correction terms.
  • Computational work $O(\varepsilon^{-r})$ — how the total operation count grows as you demand accuracy $\varepsilon$; smaller exponent $r$ means a faster method.

Honest Take

This is careful numerical analysis, not a new market model — it makes an existing, well-regarded model (rough Heston) practical rather than changing what it predicts. The error rates rest on regularity and decay assumptions that hold for the tested parameter ranges but could degrade for extreme roughness or very short maturities. For a desk already committed to rough vol it is directly useful; for everyone else it's an efficiency upgrade to machinery they may not run.

Bottom Line

It doesn't make rough-volatility pricing more accurate — it makes it affordable, by spending compute only where the answer actually needs it.

2. Stochastic Optimal Control of Hawkes Jump-Diffusion Systems

Daria Sakhanda, Joshué Helí Ricalde-Guerrero
math.OC · 2026-08-30 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When disasters make more disasters more likely, the standard "random rare shocks" math understates the danger — this paper builds the control theory for self-exciting catastrophes and proves exactly when you can safely ignore the extra clustering.

Plain English

The problem. Imagine you're steering an economy (or any system that grows over time) while catastrophes — pandemics, financial crashes, climate disasters — occasionally strike and destroy some of your accumulated capital. A standard way to model "rare bad events" treats them as a Poisson process: shocks fall like independent raindrops, each one unaware of the others. That's mathematically convenient but wrong in an important way: real disasters cluster. One bank failure raises the odds of the next; one wildfire primes the landscape for more. A shock today makes shocks tomorrow more likely.

The idea. The authors swap the "independent raindrops" assumption for a Hawkes process — a model where each event temporarily bumps up the arrival rate of future events, which then decays back down. Picture aftershocks following an earthquake: the ground stays twitchy for a while. They then ask the planner's question: given this scarier, clustering risk, how should you optimally invest and manage pollution to maximize long-run welfare? And crucially — how wrong were you if you used the simpler independent model instead? Their answer is a "dial": they introduce a knob controlling how strongly past disasters excite future ones, and prove that as you turn that knob toward zero, the sophisticated Hawkes answer smoothly collapses back onto the simple Poisson answer — with a quantified error.

What they found. They establish that the controlled system is well-posed — it doesn't blow up to infinite disasters in finite time — and they prove that under "small self-excitation," the optimal-value function of the hard Hawkes problem converges to the easy Poisson one, with explicit estimates on the gap. So they've both built the harder machinery and told you precisely when the easier machinery is a safe approximation.

The Math

The setup. The state is capital $K_t$ and pollution $P_t$, evolving by controlled dynamics and hit by disasters. Disasters arrive via a marked Hawkes process with stochastic intensity $$\lambda_t = \lambda_0 + \int_0^t \varphi(t-s)\,dN_s,$$ where $\lambda_t$ is the instantaneous jump-arrival rate, $\lambda_0$ a baseline, $N_s$ the counting process of past disasters, and $\varphi$ a decaying excitation kernel — each past jump raises the current rate by $\varphi(t-s)$. They specialize to a subcritical Markovian kernel (exponential decay), which lets them augment the state with a self-excitation variable $Z_t$ so the whole thing becomes Markov.

The key equation. The planner maximizes expected discounted utility, and the value function $V$ solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation with a nonlocal jump term: $$\rho V(x) = \sup_{c}\Big\{ f(x,c) + \mathcal{L}^c V(x) + \lambda(x)\,\mathbb{E}\big[V(x+\Gamma) - V(x)\big] \Big\},$$ where $\rho$ is the discount rate, $c$ the control (consumption/abatement), $f$ the instantaneous reward, $\mathcal{L}^c$ the diffusion generator, $\lambda(x)$ the state-dependent Hawkes intensity, and $\Gamma$ the random jump mark (the size of the catastrophe's damage). The last term captures the expected value change across a disaster.

The method. They use stochastic optimal control and the HJB characterization: prove existence and non-explosion of the controlled Hawkes dynamics, then do a perturbation analysis. Writing the excitation magnitude as a small parameter $\eta$, they derive quantitative estimates on the Hawkes excitation process and show $$\big|\,V^{\text{Hawkes}} - V^{\text{Poisson}}\,\big| \le C\,\eta$$ as $\eta \to 0$ — a rigorous Poisson approximation with a linear-in-$\eta$ error bound. Constant $C$ depends on the model primitives.

Results. The headline is qualitative-but-rigorous: well-posedness and nonexplosion of the augmented controlled system, plus the convergence theorem quantifying how self-excitation distorts optimal growth. There's no calibrated Sharpe ratio here — the "numbers" are convergence rates and the explicit dependence of the value gap on the excitation scale.

Jargon Decoded

  • Poisson process — a model of events arriving independently at a constant average rate, with no memory of past events.
  • Hawkes process — a "self-exciting" point process where each event temporarily raises the arrival rate of future events, capturing clustering.
  • Excitation kernel $\varphi$ — the function describing how much, and for how long, a past event boosts the current arrival rate.
  • Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation — the master equation of optimal control; its solution, the value function, encodes the best achievable payoff from every state.
  • Value function — the maximum expected discounted reward obtainable starting from a given state, over all admissible control strategies.
  • Subcritical / non-explosion — a stability condition ensuring the self-excitation feedback stays bounded so only finitely many events occur in finite time.

Honest Take

This is foundational theory: it builds and validates machinery rather than delivering a plug-in trading result. The convergence guarantee only bites in the small-excitation regime — precisely the regime where clustering matters least, so the hardest, most dangerous highly-clustered cases (think cascading financial crises) are exactly where the reassuring Poisson approximation is not justified. Practitioners get a clean warning label and a solid framework, but not yet calibrated numbers for a real crisis.

Bottom Line

Disasters that breed more disasters need their own control theory — and this paper draws the exact line beyond which pretending they're independent stops being safe.

3. Optimal Block Time for AMM Liquidity Providers under Jump-Diffusion Prices

Nils Bundi
q-fin.MF · 2026-08-31 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

Everyone races to make blockchain blocks faster to protect the people who provide trading liquidity — but once you admit prices can jump, most of their losses don't shrink with speed at all, so the race has a hard floor.

Plain English

The problem. On a decentralized exchange like Uniswap, "liquidity providers" (LPs) deposit two assets into a pool and let a formula set the price automatically. That's an automated market maker (AMM). The trouble: whenever the true market price moves, arbitrageurs trade against the stale pool price and skim a profit — a loss to the LP called Loss-versus-Rebalancing (LVR), the dominant cost of being an LP. A popular argument says: shrink the block time $\Delta t$ (how often the blockchain updates) toward zero and this loss vanishes, because there's less time for the price to drift out of line between updates. This has been used to justify ever-faster chains like Solana.

The idea. That argument secretly assumes prices move smoothly (a diffusion — lots of tiny steps). But real crypto prices jump — a big news event moves the price instantly, no matter how fast your blocks are. The author re-does the LVR calculation allowing jumps, and finds the loss splits into two independent "channels," like two leaks in a boat: a diffusion channel that does shrink as blocks get faster, and a jump channel that doesn't care about block speed at all. Speeding up the chain plugs one leak while the other keeps flowing. So there's a floor: no matter how fast you go, LPs keep bleeding from the jumps.

What they found. With Ethereum's 12-second blocks, the LVR rate is about 471 basis points per year, but 125 of those (about a quarter of the loss) come from the jump channel and can't be fixed by faster blocks. On Solana's 400-millisecond blocks the jump channel already dominates. And the LP-optimal block time — once you net trading loss against the cost of running consensus each block — turns out to be about 8 seconds, and remarkably it doesn't depend on the pool size or any of the jump parameters; only volatility, the fee tier, and consensus cost move it.

The Math

The setup. The reference price follows a jump-diffusion: $$\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = \sigma\,dW_t + dJ_t,$$ where $S_t$ is the price, $\sigma$ the volatility, $W_t$ a Brownian motion (the smooth part), and $J_t$ a compound-Poisson jump term with intensity $\lambda$ (jumps per unit time) and log-jump sizes drawn from a law with mean $m$ and variance $\delta^2$. The AMM is constant-product ($x\cdot y = k$). Arbitrageurs realign the pool to the reference at the start of each block of length $\Delta t$.

The key equation. The instantaneous LVR rate decomposes as $$\ell(\Delta t) = \underbrace{\tfrac12 \sigma^2\, F\!\Big(\tfrac{\gamma}{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\Big)}_{\text{diffusion channel}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda\,V\cdot G(\gamma; m,\delta^2)}_{\text{jump channel}} \;+\; R(\Delta t),$$ where $\gamma$ is the pool's fee/slippage parameter, $F$ a known multiplier function of the single ratio $\gamma/(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t})$, $\lambda V\,G$ the jump contribution ($V$ a pool-value scale, $G$ a function of the jump law), and $R$ an explicitly bounded interaction remainder. Crucially the jump channel carries no $\Delta t$.

The method. This is an asymptotic expansion of the arbitrage value in the small-$\Delta t$ regime, with a rigorously bounded remainder isolating the two channels. For symmetric jump laws he proves the jump channel is an exact lower bound, $$\ell(\Delta t) \ge \lambda V\,G > 0,$$ so the rate cannot vanish as $\Delta t\to 0$ and only decays like $\sqrt{\Delta t}$. Netting against a per-block consensus cost gives a first-order condition for the LP-optimal $\Delta t^\star$.

Results with numbers. Ethereum 12 s slot: 471 bp/yr total LVR against a 125 bp/yr irreducible floor — only ~75% of loss is "schedule-addressable." Solana 400 ms: jump channel already dominant. LP-optimal block time $\approx 8$ s, invariant in pool size and in every jump parameter $(\lambda, m, \delta)$; set instead by $\sigma$, fee tier, and consensus cost.

Jargon Decoded

  • Automated market maker (AMM) — an exchange that prices trades by a fixed formula on pooled reserves rather than by matching buyers and sellers.
  • Loss-versus-Rebalancing (LVR) — the systematic profit arbitrageurs extract from an AMM's stale prices, equal to the LP's adverse-selection loss.
  • Block time $\Delta t$ — how often the blockchain finalizes a new batch of transactions and thus how often the pool price can be corrected.
  • Jump-diffusion — a price model combining continuous Brownian wiggles with sudden discrete jumps.
  • Constant-product AMM — the Uniswap-style rule that keeps the product of the two reserve amounts fixed, $x\cdot y=k$.
  • Basis point (bp) — one hundredth of a percent; 471 bp/yr means 4.71% of pool value lost per year.

Honest Take

The result is a clean lower bound on one cost, not a full welfare verdict — the author is explicit that LVR is only one input to block-time design, so this bounds the LP-side contribution rather than settling how fast a chain "should" be. It leans on a jump-diffusion with a specific (symmetric) jump law and on frictionless, instantaneous arbitrage each block; real MEV dynamics, competition among arbitrageurs, and fee rebates could shift the numbers. Still, the qualitative message — that faster blocks hit a floor — is robust and genuinely useful for protocol designers.

Bottom Line

You can outrun the smooth losses but not the jumps, so the crusade for ever-faster blocks protects liquidity providers only up to a point — and that point is around eight seconds.

4. Neural Calibration of a Complete Market Model

Andrea Molent, Michel Vellekoop
q-fin.CP · 2026-08-31 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

Instead of guessing a smooth volatility surface and hoping it prices options right, this paper lets a neural network gently bend a simple option-pricing tree until it matches the market — and the bent tree is guaranteed to never allow a free lunch.

Plain English

The problem. To price and hedge options you need a model calibrated to today's market prices. The usual pipeline estimates an intermediate object — a local volatility surface, a function telling you how volatile the stock is at every price and time — and then prices off it. But that surface is a delicate, continuous thing; small estimation wobbles can create arbitrage, i.e. prices that let someone lock in a riskless profit, which is both unrealistic and dangerous to trade on. And a fitted surface doesn't directly hand you a hedging strategy.

The idea. The authors skip the surface entirely. Picture a binomial tree: a simple stick-figure model of the future where, each step, the price either ticks up or down, branching into a lattice of possible paths. A plain tree is crude but has a magic property — it's complete and arbitrage-free by construction, meaning every option can be exactly replicated by trading the stock and cash, and no free lunch exists. The trick: start from a sensible benchmark tree and let a neural network (a flexible function-fitter) deform it — nudge the up/down moves and probabilities — until the tree reprices the observed options accurately. Because you're deforming a genuine tree rather than inventing prices, the result stays arbitrage-free and complete automatically. It's like re-shaping a clay model that's guaranteed to remain a valid object no matter how you push it.

What they found. On both synthetic tests and real SPX (S&P 500) option data, the deformed tree reprices options accurately and is competitive with other recent neural calibration methods — while keeping the huge practical advantages of trees: you can directly read off hedging (replication) strategies, and, because trees handle early exercise naturally, the same calibrated tree can price American options too.

The Math

The setup. A recombining binomial tree on a time grid $t_0<\dots

The key equation. A neural network $\mathcal{N}_\theta$ with weights $\theta$ deforms the benchmark lattice, outputting the node prices and transition probabilities. Calibration solves a penalized optimization: $$\min_{\theta}\ \underbrace{\sum_k \big(V^{\mathcal{N}_\theta}_k - V^{\text{mkt}}_k\big)^2}_{\text{repricing error}} \;+\; \alpha\,\mathcal{P}_{\text{adm}}(\theta) \;+\; \beta\,\mathcal{R}_{\text{loc-vol}}(\theta),$$ where $V^{\mathcal{N}_\theta}_k$ is the model price of the $k$-th quoted option, $V^{\text{mkt}}_k$ its market price, $\mathcal{P}_{\text{adm}}$ an admissibility penalty enforcing valid probabilities $q_{i,j}\in[0,1]$, $\mathcal{R}_{\text{loc-vol}}$ an optional spatial regularizer pulling the implied local volatilities toward smoothness, and $\alpha,\beta\ge 0$ their weights.

The method. This is neural calibration by direct model construction: gradient-descent training (backpropagation) of the network so the tree it produces reprices the market, with the admissibility penalty guaranteeing the output is a legitimate — hence arbitrage-free and complete — pricing lattice. No separate no-arbitrage repair step is needed because the parameterization can't leave the arbitrage-free set once $q_{i,j}\in[0,1]$.

Results with numbers. On synthetic data (where ground truth is known) and on SPX market quotes, the method achieves accurate repricing and is very competitive against recently proposed neural calibration approaches, while retaining lattice-based valuation/hedging speed. Concretely, the calibrated tree is reusable to price early-exercise (American/Bermudan) contracts, and could in principle be calibrated directly to American option prices — a flexibility the surface-first methods lack.

Jargon Decoded

  • Local volatility surface — a function giving the stock's instantaneous volatility as it depends on price level and time; the classic intermediate object in calibration.
  • Binomial tree — a discrete price model where each step branches into an up-move or a down-move, forming a lattice of scenarios.
  • Recombining / complete market — recombining keeps up-down = down-up so the tree stays small; complete means every payoff can be exactly replicated by trading, giving unique prices.
  • Arbitrage-free — no strategy earns a riskless profit from nothing; the defining sanity condition for any pricing model.
  • Backward induction — computing option values from maturity back to today, one layer at a time, using discounted expected future values.
  • Admissibility penalty — an extra loss term that punishes parameter choices producing invalid (e.g. out-of-range) probabilities, keeping the model well-formed.

Honest Take

Trees are transparent and hedging-friendly, but they're also coarse — matching a rich, dense option surface may demand fine grids, and the paper's edge is "competitive," not a dramatic accuracy leap over rival neural methods. The guarantee of no-arbitrage is real and valuable, yet it's a discrete-model guarantee; how faithfully the lattice captures true continuous dynamics (and behaves out-of-sample as markets move) is the practical question. For a desk that values interpretability and direct American-option pricing, this is genuinely attractive.

Bottom Line

By bending a guaranteed-valid tree instead of guessing a fragile surface, you get a calibrated model that's arbitrage-free by construction and hands you the hedge for free.

5. The Convergence Rate of Stochastic Tracking with Application to Optimal Execution

Marcel Nutz, Moritz Voss
q-fin.TR · 2026-08-29 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When you must quietly buy a huge block of stock while chasing a moving target, how closely can you track it — and this paper pins down the exact best-possible rate, then builds a strategy that nearly achieves it.

Plain English

The problem. Suppose a pension fund needs to buy a million shares. Dump them all at once and you'll shove the price up against yourself — that extra cost is price impact. So you spread the order out over the day. But you're also trying to track a target: maybe you want your holdings to follow a signal, or to end the day at a specific inventory. This is a tug-of-war — trade fast and you pay impact, trade slow and you drift away from your target. The classic Obizhaeva–Wang model formalizes optimal execution with impact that builds up and slowly decays, like pressing a mattress that springs back. Two questions have nagged: how well can any strategy track a general, random target, and what happens when you insist on smooth trading (no sudden bursts)?

The idea. Think of tracking a wandering target like steering a heavy truck to follow a nervous cyclist. If the cyclist can swerve sharply, you can't match every twitch — your tracking error is limited by how rough the cyclist's path is. The authors make this precise: they bound how well you can track any stochastic target purely in terms of a mathematical measure of the target's roughness, without assuming anything special about it. Then, because the truly optimal execution strategy turns out to want instantaneous jumps (impractical trading spikes), they add a gentle penalty on trading speed to smooth it out — and ask how much that smoothing costs. Their answer: surprisingly little, and they quantify exactly how the cost shrinks as you allow the strategy to get sharper.

What they found. For "semimartingale" targets (a broad, realistic class) the best achievable tracking error scales like the square root of the time resolution — a clean, sharp rate. And when you regularize the spiky optimal execution strategy with a small speed-penalty of size $\varepsilon$, the extra cost you pay vanishes at rate $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. Better still, since the exact optimal strategy isn't available in closed form, they hand you a nearly optimal, actually implementable strategy achieving the same rate.

The Math

The setup. A quadratic tracking problem: choose an absolutely continuous control (your holdings path) $X_t$ with rate $\dot X_t$ to follow a target process $\xi_t$, minimizing $$J = \mathbb{E}\!\int_0^T \big(X_t - \xi_t\big)^2\,dt \;+\; (\text{impact/penalty terms}),$$ with and without a terminal constraint $X_T = \text{const}$. Here $X_t$ is your position, $\xi_t$ the (random) target, $T$ the horizon, and $\dot X_t$ the trading rate. The target's roughness is measured by a Besov-type modulus, a functional gauging how much $\xi$ can oscillate at scale $h$.

The key equations. They prove non-asymptotic upper bounds on the optimal tracking error in terms of that Besov modulus, which specialize for semimartingale targets to the sharp rate $$\text{error} = O(h^{1/2}),$$ where $h$ is the time resolution. Applied to a generalized Obizhaeva–Wang execution model with random terminal inventory, the optimal strategy is characterized via a Hilbert-space formulation and generically contains jumps. Adding a quadratic trading-rate penalty with coefficient $\varepsilon$, $$J_\varepsilon = J + \varepsilon \int_0^T \dot X_t^2\,dt,$$ regularizes those jumps away. They then show the regularized optimal cost — hence the excess price-impact cost of the regularized strategy — converges at the sharp rate $$\big|\,C_\varepsilon - C_0\,\big| = O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}),$$ where $C_\varepsilon$ is the regularized minimal cost, $C_0$ the unregularized optimum, and $\varepsilon$ the penalty coefficient.

The method. The engine is functional analysis and stochastic calculus: recast the execution problem in a Hilbert space so the optimal control is the projection characterized by a first-order condition; derive Besov-modulus bounds by controlling the target's oscillation; and prove the $O(\sqrt\varepsilon)$ regularization rate. Finally they construct an explicit near-optimal strategy (not just prove one exists) that is readily implementable and shares the same $\sqrt\varepsilon$ approximation rate.

Results with numbers. The headline numbers are convergence rates, and they are sharp (matching lower and upper bounds): square-root order $O(h^{1/2})$ tracking error for semimartingale targets, and $O(\sqrt\varepsilon)$ excess execution cost from regularization. "Sharp" means the exponent cannot be improved.

Jargon Decoded

  • Optimal execution — the problem of trading a large order over time to minimize total cost, chiefly price impact.
  • Price impact (Obizhaeva–Wang model) — the effect that your own trading moves the price; in this model impact accumulates and then decays exponentially, like a transient shock.
  • Quadratic tracking — steering a controlled process to stay close to a target by penalizing the squared distance between them.
  • Semimartingale — a broad class of stochastic processes (drift plus martingale) covering essentially all standard financial price models.
  • Besov modulus — a fine-grained measure of a function or path's roughness/oscillation across scales, sharper than ordinary smoothness classes.
  • Regularization coefficient $\varepsilon$ — the weight on a penalty (here on trading speed) that smooths an otherwise spiky optimal strategy; smaller $\varepsilon$ means closer to the raw optimum.

Honest Take

This is rigorous stochastic-control theory, and its value is precisely its sharpness — it tells a practitioner how much smoothing costs, so you can tune $\varepsilon$ knowingly rather than by trial. But the impact model is the stylized Obizhaeva–Wang setup (linear, transient impact); real order books have nonlinear, fleeting, and adversarial impact the theory doesn't capture, and the near-optimal strategy still assumes you know the target's dynamics. It's a precise map of an idealized landscape.

Bottom Line

You can't perfectly chase a jittery target, but this paper pins down exactly how close you can get — and gives you a smooth, buildable strategy that gets there.
