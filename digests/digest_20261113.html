1. Price-impact Martingale optimal transport

Erhan Bayraktar, Shuoqing Deng, Daxin Huang, Dominykas Norgilas
q-fin.MF · 2026-11-12 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

If trading the stock itself nudges its price, the model-free "cheapest-to-hedge" price of an option is no longer a single number but a band that widens with how much you have to trade.

Plain English

The problem. Suppose you sell a friend an exotic bet on a stock — say, a payout that depends on where the stock lands in three months. You want to charge enough that, by trading the stock in the meantime, you can cover whatever you owe no matter what the market does. The classic question of "what is the fair price?" has a beautiful model-free answer: if you know the prices of ordinary ("vanilla") call options today, those prices pin down the range of possible future outcomes, and that range gives you upper and lower bounds on the exotic's price — without committing to any particular model of how the stock moves. The catch in the standard story is a fairy-tale assumption: that you can trade any quantity instantly at the quoted price. In reality, buying a lot pushes the price up and selling a lot pushes it down. This "price impact" is exactly what a big fund worries about, and ignoring it gives bounds that are too tight to trust.

The idea. The authors rebuild the pricing bounds while charging you for the disturbance your own trades create. Picture the frictionless theory as assuming the stock price is a perfectly balanced spinning top (a "martingale" — no predictable drift). Price impact knocks the top slightly off balance every time you touch it. Instead of forcing perfect balance, the authors penalize imbalance: you are allowed to disturb the price, but each disturbance costs you, quadratically (twice the trade, four times the cost). Mathematically this turns the problem into a cousin of "optimal transport" — the math of moving a pile of sand into a target shape at least cost — but a weak version where what matters is the average destination of each grain, not each grain's exact path. They show their price-impact problem is a projection, in a precise geometric sense, onto the set of balanced distributions.

What they found. Their new bounds smoothly interpolate between two classical extremes: when impact vanishes, you recover the old frictionless model-free bounds; when impact is enormous (trading is prohibitively costly), you recover plain optimal transport with no martingale/balance condition at all. They prove exactly how fast you move from one regime to the other as impact changes. On real S&P 500 option data, they show the price band per option contract genuinely widens as the order size grows — quantifying, for the first time in this framework, the premium you should demand for size.

The Math

The setup. Fix two dates and let $\mu$ be the law (probability distribution) of the stock price today and $\nu$ its law at maturity; by the Breeden–Litzenberger relation these marginals are read off from vanilla option prices. Classical martingale optimal transport (MOT) searches over couplings $\pi$ of $\mu$ and $\nu$ — joint distributions with these marginals — subject to the martingale constraint $$\int y\,\pi(dy\mid x) = x \quad \text{for } \mu\text{-a.e. } x,$$ i.e. the conditional mean of the future price given today's price $x$ equals $x$ (no free drift). The price bound for a payoff $c(x,y)$ is $\sup_\pi \int c\,d\pi$ (upper) or $\inf_\pi$ (lower).

The key equations. With quadratic temporary price impact of strength $\lambda>0$, the authors relax the hard constraint into a penalty, giving the primal price-impact MOT value $$V_\lambda = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int c(x,y)\,\pi(dx,dy) \;+\; \frac{1}{2\lambda}\int \big(\,\overline{m}(x) - x\,\big)^2\,\mu(dx),$$ where $\overline{m}(x) = \int y\,\pi(dy\mid x)$ is the conditional mean (the "barycenter" of where mass starting at $x$ is sent), and the squared deviation $(\overline{m}(x)-x)^2$ is the penalty for breaking the balance condition. Here $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is the set of couplings with the given marginals, $\lambda$ scales the impact cost, and $c$ is the hedging payoff. Because the penalty depends only on $\pi$ through the conditional law $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$, this is a weak optimal transport (WOT) problem; the authors characterize the optimizer as a Wasserstein projection of a reference measure onto the martingale-like set.

The method. They prove a superhedging duality: the primal above equals a dual over trading strategies and static vanilla positions, with the martingale term replaced by its penalized Legendre transform. For one asset and one period they reduce everything to a one-dimensional projection and solve it explicitly. The interpolation claims are proved by sending $\lambda \to 0$ (recovering MOT) and $\lambda \to \infty$ (recovering classical OT), with explicit convergence rates to each limit.

Results with numbers. On SPX option data they compute upper/lower price bounds as a function of order size; the headline qualitative result is that the per-claim bid–ask-like band is monotone increasing in the traded quantity, and collapses to the frictionless MOT band as order size (hence required trading) shrinks to zero.

Jargon Decoded

  • Martingale — a process with no predictable drift: its expected future value equals its current value.
  • Optimal transport — the problem of rearranging one probability distribution into another at minimum total "moving" cost.
  • Martingale optimal transport (MOT) — optimal transport with the extra rule that the coupling must be a martingale, which is what makes the price bounds model-free.
  • Weak optimal transport (WOT) — a relaxation where the cost depends only on the average destination of mass from each source point, not the full coupling.
  • Superhedging — covering a liability for sure, in every scenario, by trading; its cheapest cost is the upper price bound.
  • Breeden–Litzenberger — the fact that the second derivative of call prices in strike recovers the risk-neutral distribution of the underlying.

Honest Take

The clean results live in the one-asset, one-period world; multi-period and multi-asset price impact — where today's trades distort tomorrow's state — is where real execution desks live, and that is left open. Quadratic temporary impact is a convenient caricature of a messier, often concave ("square-root law") reality, and the framework still inherits optimal transport's appetite for well-estimated marginals. Still, as a conceptual bridge it is genuinely useful: it tells a desk how much of its model-free pricing comfort it should surrender simply because its own orders move the tape.

Bottom Line

Model-free option bounds stop being a fixed interval and become a function of how much you trade, with impact dialing continuously between frictionless pricing and pure optimal transport.

2. Convergence rates for truncated and discretised signature expansions

Eduardo Abi Jaber, Dorinel Bastide, Ronan Pécheul, Clément Rey
math.PR · 2026-11-11 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When you summarize a bumpy, memory-laden price path by its "signature" and then chop that summary off at a finite level and sample the path at finitely many times, this paper tells you exactly how wrong you are — and the error shrinks almost factorially fast.

Plain English

The problem. Many modern finance models have memory: today's volatility remembers how rough yesterday was, interest-rate curves drag their history along, and "rough volatility" models deliberately build in long-range dependence to match market data. These processes are awkward because they are not Markovian — you cannot summarize the past in a single current number. A powerful trick from stochastic analysis is the path signature: instead of describing a wiggly path by its endpoint, you describe it by an entire hierarchy of "iterated integrals" — roughly, how much the path moves, then how its movements compound in pairs, in triples, and so on. This is like describing a probability distribution not by its mean alone but by all its moments. Remarkably, many quantities you care about (like an option price) can be written as a simple linear combination of signature terms. The practical snag: the signature is infinitely long and lives on a continuous path, but a computer can only keep finitely many terms (truncate at level $M$) and can only see the path at finitely many time steps (discretise into $N$ pieces). How much accuracy does that cost?

The idea. The authors nail down both errors with sharp mathematical bounds, the way a numerical analyst bounds the error of rounding and the error of a grid separately. Think of two independent dials: one is "how many signature levels do I keep" ($M$), the other is "how finely do I sample the path" ($N$). They prove you can turn these dials independently, and they give the exact decay law for each.

What they found. Keeping more signature levels buys you accuracy astonishingly fast — faster than any power of $M$, essentially factorially. Sampling the path more finely buys you accuracy at a steadier polynomial rate. Crucially the two choices don't interfere, so you can pick the cheapest combination for a target accuracy. They extend the guarantees beyond averages to whole distribution functions and even to risk measures (the tail quantities regulators care about), and they demonstrate it on stochastic delay equations, Volterra processes, and option pricing in signature-based volatility models — exactly the memory-laden settings where this machinery earns its keep.

The Math

The setup. Let $X=(X_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ be a driving path (e.g. Brownian motion in Stratonovich form). Its signature is the collection of iterated Stratonovich integrals $$\mathbb{S}(X)^{(i_1,\dots,i_k)}_{0,t} = \int_{0linear signature functional approximates a quantity of interest as $$F_t \approx \sum_{|w|\le M} a_w\, \mathbb{S}(X)^{w}_{0,t},$$ where $w$ ranges over words up to length $M$, and $a_w$ are real coefficients from a stochastic Taylor expansion.

The key equations. Under a growth condition on the coefficients, controlled by a parameter $\varepsilon$ (how fast the $a_w$ grow) and $a$ (the regularity of the test function), they prove the strong truncation error obeys $$\big\| F_t - F_t^{(M)} \big\|_{L^p} \;\lesssim\; \Big(\tfrac{B^{M}}{(M!)^{\varepsilon}}\Big)^{a},$$ uniformly in time and in $N$, where $B$ is a constant, $M!$ is the factorial of the truncation level, and $F_t^{(M)}$ is the level-$M$ truncation. The discretisation error from replacing the continuous path by $N$ steps is $$\big\| F_t - F_t^{(N\text{-disc})} \big\|_{L^p} \;\lesssim\; N^{-a/2},$$ with a sharper weak rate $N^{-1}$ for polynomial test functions, uniformly in $M$.

The method. The proofs combine stochastic Taylor (Wagner–Platen-type) expansions with factorial decay estimates for iterated integrals and $L^p$ moment bounds, plus Lipschitz-transfer arguments to push error bounds from expectations onto distribution functions and risk measures. The decoupling of $M$ and $N$ is the key structural result.

Results with numbers. The rates are proven sharp for polynomial test functions (the exponents cannot be improved), and $M,N$ are shown to be selectable independently. Numerical illustrations on stochastic delay equations, Volterra processes, and signature volatility option pricing confirm the predicted $N^{-a/2}$ (strong) / $N^{-1}$ (weak) discretisation decay and the near-factorial truncation decay.

Jargon Decoded

  • Path signature — an infinite hierarchy of iterated integrals of a path that captures its geometry and order of movements, like moments capture a distribution.
  • Stratonovich integral — a version of stochastic integration obeying ordinary calculus' chain rule, which is why signatures behave algebraically.
  • Truncation level $M$ — the longest "word" (nesting depth) of iterated integrals you keep before discarding higher terms.
  • Strong vs. weak convergence — strong bounds the pathwise error size ($L^p$); weak bounds the error only in averages of smooth functions, and is usually faster.
  • Volterra / rough volatility process — a process whose present depends on a weighted average of its whole past, giving long memory and non-Markovian dynamics.
  • Risk measure — a number summarizing the downside of a distribution (e.g. Value-at-Risk), i.e. a tail functional, not just a mean.

Honest Take

These are clean, sharp approximation-theory results, but they assume you already have the coefficients $a_w$ of the signature expansion and that they satisfy the growth condition — fitting those coefficients from data, and in high dimensions where the number of words explodes combinatorially, is the practical bottleneck this paper does not address. The factorial truncation decay is gorgeous for low dimensions and smooth payoffs; for kinked or discontinuous payoffs (barriers, digitals) the regularity parameter $a$ degrades and the comfort shrinks. As a rigorous foundation for signature-based pricing libraries, though, it is exactly the kind of guarantee practitioners should want before trusting the method on rough-volatility books.

Bottom Line

Signatures can represent memory-laden price processes with error that collapses almost factorially in how much you keep and polynomially in how finely you look — and now we know those rates are the best possible.

3. Optimal execution in a fractional setting with reinforcement learning

Matthieu Garcin, Jiang Pu, Thomas Verrier
q-fin.TR · 2026-11-08 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When prices have memory, the textbook "sell a big position on a fixed schedule" answer is leaving money on the table, and a neural-net trader that reads the memory can do measurably better.

Plain English

The problem. Imagine you must sell a million shares of a stock by the end of the day. Dump them all at once and you crash the price against yourself; dribble them out too slowly and you risk the price drifting away before you finish. This is the optimal execution problem, and the famous Almgren–Chriss solution from 2000 gives an elegant answer: assuming the price is a pure random walk (each tick independent of the last), the best plan is a deterministic schedule you can write down in advance — trade a bit faster or slower depending only on how nervous you are about risk, never on what the price actually does. That "independent ticks" assumption is the quiet lie. Real price moves are correlated: trends persist, or reversals cluster, depending on the asset. If you know the next move is slightly more likely up than down, you should obviously wait; if a reversal is brewing, sell now. A deterministic schedule throws that information away.

The idea. The authors keep everything about Almgren–Chriss — the same cost of impact, the same risk aversion — but replace the random-walk engine with fractional Brownian motion, a noise with a tunable memory knob called the Hurst exponent $H$. When $H=1/2$ you are back to the memoryless random walk and Almgren–Chriss is optimal; when $H>1/2$ moves trend (momentum), and when $H<1/2$ they mean-revert (reversal). In these memory regimes the old deterministic schedule is provably no longer optimal, because you can exploit the correlation between past and future increments. Rather than solve the (hard, non-Markovian) control problem by hand, they train a neural network to be the trading policy, nudging it by trial and error — "policy gradient," the same family of reinforcement-learning methods behind game-playing agents — to maximize the trader's happiness.

What they found. The learned policy adapts to the path: it leans on the observed drift that memory creates, speeding up or slowing down in response to realized moves rather than following a fixed clock. Against the Almgren–Chriss benchmark it delivers significant improvements in the trader's expected utility whenever $H\neq 1/2$ — i.e. whenever the market actually has the memory that real markets display.

The Math

The setup. The trader holds inventory $q_t$ starting at $q_0$ and must reach $q_T=0$ by horizon $T$, trading at rate $v_t = -\dot q_t$. The mid-price follows a fractional dynamics $$dS_t = \sigma\,dW^H_t - \kappa\, v_t\,dt,$$ where $W^H$ is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent $H\in(0,1)$, $\sigma$ is volatility, and $\kappa\, v_t$ is the permanent impact of trading at rate $v_t$. The execution price adds a temporary impact $\eta\, v_t$, so cash accrues at rate $(S_t - \eta v_t)\,v_t$. The defining feature of $W^H$ is its correlated increments: its covariance is $$\mathbb{E}\big[W^H_t W^H_s\big] = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H}\big),$$ which reduces to $\min(t,s)$ (independent increments) exactly when $H=1/2$.

The key equations. The trader maximizes CARA (exponential) utility of terminal wealth $X_T$, $$\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}\big[\, U(X_T)\,\big], \qquad U(x) = -\exp(-\gamma x),$$ where $\gamma>0$ is the risk-aversion coefficient and the policy $\pi_\theta$ maps the observed state (time, inventory, recent price history) to a trading rate via a neural network with parameters $\theta$. They optimize by policy gradient, $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v\mid \text{state})\,\big(U(X_T)-b\big)\big],$$ with $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[U(X_T)]$ the objective and $b$ a baseline that reduces variance of the estimate.

The method. Because $W^H$ is non-Markovian for $H\neq 1/2$, no finite-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation applies; the authors sidestep this by model-free reinforcement learning — simulate many fractional price paths, let the network trade them, and ascend the gradient of expected utility. The network input includes realized price increments, so it can infer the conditional drift that memory induces.

Results with numbers. For $H=1/2$ the learned policy reproduces the Almgren–Chriss deterministic trajectory, a sanity check. For $H\neq 1/2$ the paper reports significant gains in expected CARA utility over the Almgren–Chriss baseline, with the policy visibly conditioning its trading rate on the sign and size of recent increments rather than on time alone.

Jargon Decoded

  • Optimal execution — the problem of unwinding a large position over time to balance price impact against timing risk.
  • Almgren–Chriss — the classical model whose optimal liquidation schedule is deterministic under random-walk prices.
  • Fractional Brownian motion — a Gaussian process whose increments are correlated, with memory controlled by the Hurst exponent.
  • Hurst exponent $H$ — the memory dial: $H>1/2$ trending, $H<1/2$ mean-reverting, $H=1/2$ memoryless.
  • Temporary vs. permanent impact — temporary impact moves only your own fill price and relaxes; permanent impact shifts the market price for everyone.
  • CARA utility — exponential utility $-e^{-\gamma x}$, encoding constant absolute risk aversion $\gamma$.
  • Policy gradient — a reinforcement-learning method that improves a parameterized policy by ascending the gradient of expected reward.

Honest Take

The gains are demonstrated on simulated fractional paths where the memory is real by construction; whether a traded asset's Hurst exponent is stable, estimable intraday, and large enough to beat transaction costs is an empirical question the paper does not settle. Reinforcement-learning execution also tends to overfit the simulator, and a policy that learned to lean into trends can be dangerous precisely when regimes flip. Treat it as a proof that memory is exploitable in principle, not as a plug-in replacement for a production execution algo.

Bottom Line

If prices remember their past, so should your liquidation strategy — and a policy-gradient trader that does beats the fixed-schedule textbook answer.

4. Media Trading with Censored Auction Prices: A Stochastic Control and Reinforcement Learning Approach

Yun-Ithry Gamrani, Huyên Pham
q-fin.MF · 2026-11-12 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When you only learn the winning price of an auction by winning it, a bidder has to deliberately bid to learn as well as to win — and this paper works out how to balance the two optimally.

Plain English

The problem. Every time you load a web page with ads, a lightning-fast auction decides which ad you see, and advertisers' agents bid in real time — billions of times a day. An agency running these bids faces three tensions at once: hit the campaign's performance target, keep a margin on each transaction, and not blow a fixed budget before the campaign ends. The deep difficulty is informational. In a second-price-style auction you only discover the market-clearing price when your bid wins — lose, and you learn nothing except "it was higher than I bid." Economists call this censored data. Worse, the data you collect depends on how you bid: bid timidly and you win cheap slots but stay ignorant of the true price level; bid aggressively and you learn the price but overpay. Your strategy shapes your own information — a feedback loop that ordinary "estimate then optimize" pipelines handle badly.

The idea. The authors treat this as a stochastic control problem with a hidden ("latent") price that drifts over time, and they get clever about learning. To pierce the censoring, they run small exploratory subcampaigns in parallel where bids are deliberately randomized up to a cap — a controlled experiment that reveals the price distribution without betting the whole budget on it. They prove this gives an honest price estimate whose error shrinks predictably as you gather more observations. Then they solve the actual bidding problem two ways: a model-based route that writes down the governing equations and solves them with a physics-informed neural network, and a model-free route that learns to bid by reinforcement learning (an entropy-regularized "actor–critic," which keeps the policy exploratory rather than prematurely certain). A key honest move is that they carefully separate three worlds: a fantasy where you know all prices, a version where you plug in estimated prices, and the genuine partially-observed problem — so you can see exactly what the censoring costs you.

What they found. On synthetic experiments the exploratory-subcampaign estimator behaves as the theory predicts (error falling like one over the number of observations), and both the model-based and model-free controllers produce sensible bidding policies that respect the budget while trading off performance and margin. The framework's main payoff is conceptual clarity: it pins down the price of not knowing prices.

The Math

The setup. Work on a filtered probability space over $[0,T]$. The market-clearing price is a latent process $P_t$ (e.g. a diffusion $dP_t = b(P_t)\,dt + \sigma(P_t)\,dW_t$) that the agency does not observe directly. The agency chooses a bid $\beta_t$; it wins the impression iff $\beta_t \ge P_t$, and only then observes $P_t$ — the censoring mechanism. Remaining budget $B_t$ is a controlled state that decreases by the paid price on each win, with $B_t \ge 0$ enforced.

The key equations. The agency maximizes expected campaign value net of spend, $$V(t,B) = \sup_{\beta}\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_t^T \big(r(\beta_s,P_s) - \text{cost}_s\big)\,ds \;\Big|\; B_t=B\right],$$ where $r$ rewards performance/margin and the cost reflects what is actually paid; $V$ is the value function. In the full-information benchmark $V$ solves a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation $$\partial_t V + \sup_{\beta}\Big\{ \mathcal{L}^{\beta} V + r(\beta,P) \Big\} = 0, \qquad V(T,\cdot)=0,$$ with $\mathcal{L}^{\beta}$ the generator of the controlled state dynamics. For the exploratory estimator built from $N$ randomized observations, they prove a mean-square-error bound $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat P_N - P)^2\big] = O\!\big(N^{-1}\big),$$ so the price estimate is consistent with the standard $1/N$ rate, where $\hat P_N$ is the estimator and $N$ the number of exploratory observations.

The method. Model-based: calibrate the latent dynamics via a forward (Fokker–Planck) equation and solve the HJB by policy iteration, approximating the value function with a physics-informed neural network (a network trained to satisfy the PDE residual). Model-free: an entropy-regularized actor–critic, where the critic learns the value and the actor learns the bidding policy, with an entropy bonus that keeps exploring so the censored information keeps flowing.

Results with numbers. Synthetic experiments confirm the $O(N^{-1})$ MSE decay of the exploratory estimator and show that both solvers recover budget-respecting policies; the paper's quantitative emphasis is the gap between full-information, estimated-price, and genuinely partially-observed value — isolating the cost of censoring.

Jargon Decoded

  • Real-time bidding — the automated auction that sells each ad impression in milliseconds as a page loads.
  • Censored observation — data you only see under some condition (here, the price only when you win), biasing naive estimates.
  • Latent process — an underlying quantity that drives outcomes but is not directly observed.
  • Stochastic control — choosing actions over time to steer a random system toward a goal optimally.
  • Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation — the partial differential equation whose solution is the optimal value function of a continuous-time control problem.
  • Physics-informed neural network — a network trained to satisfy a differential equation's residual, used here to solve the HJB.
  • Actor–critic / entropy regularization — a reinforcement-learning scheme pairing a policy ("actor") with a value estimate ("critic"), plus an entropy bonus that sustains exploration.

Honest Take

The guarantees and experiments are synthetic and lean on a "stylized cross-sectional sampling" model for the exploratory subcampaigns; whether real exchanges let you randomize bids cheaply enough, and whether the latent-price diffusion is a fair description of adversarial, non-stationary ad markets, is unproven here. Deliberately running exploratory subcampaigns also burns real budget and may dent performance in ways a campaign manager will feel. The contribution is a rigorous scaffolding for a problem usually handled by ad-hoc heuristics, not a turnkey bidder.

Bottom Line

In markets where you only see the price by paying it, optimal behavior means bidding partly to learn — and that exploration cost can now be quantified and optimized.

5. TAPES: Terminal Adjoint-Propagated Error Scheduling for Financial Diffusion Samplers

Yilie Huang
q-fin.CP · 2026-11-12 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

If you run a generative "diffusion" model only a few steps to stay fast, placing those few time-steps cleverly — tuned to the exact risk number you'll report — cuts the error more than spending the same steps evenly.

Plain English

The problem. Generative diffusion models — the same technology behind AI image generators — are increasingly used in finance to manufacture plausible market scenarios: thousands of synthetic futures for stress tests, risk dashboards, and tail analysis. To make one sample, the model numerically solves a differential equation backward from noise to a realistic outcome, and each step costs one expensive neural-network evaluation. In risk pipelines you want this fast, so you allow only a handful of steps (the jargon is "number of function evaluations," NFE). But a coarse time-grid introduces numerical error, and — this is the sting — that error doesn't average out; it systematically distorts exactly the fragile quantities risk teams live and die by: covariances between assets, the fatness of the tails, drawdown proxies. A blurry image is tolerable; a mis-estimated tail is a blown risk limit.

The idea. TAPES is a smarter way to schedule those few precious steps, decided once offline, leaving the trained model and its solver completely untouched. The insight: not all numerical error matters equally. A step-error early in the solve gets transported, reshaped, and amplified or damped as it flows through the remaining steps before it finally lands in the number you report. So TAPES estimates each step's local error (using Richardson extrapolation — compare a coarse step to two half-steps and read off the discrepancy), then propagates that error forward through the rest of the flow and through a "terminal adjoint" that encodes how sensitive your final reported statistic is to a nudge at each point. It then arranges the time-grid to make the total transported error in the thing you actually report as small as possible — rather than naively equalizing error step by step, which TAPES shows is wasteful.

What they found. Across six step-budgets, a regime-switching simulation, and held-out real contexts built from the "Magnificent 7" mega-cap stocks, TAPES gave the lowest reported error in all 24 tested combinations within its comparison panel. The average error reduction was about 10–11% on the regime-switching data and about 1% on the Mag7 data, with statistical intervals (Bonferroni-corrected) excluding zero, and the ranking held up under a doubled-resolution reference check.

The Math

The setup. Fix a trained diffusion model as a probability-flow ODE $$\frac{dx}{dt} = f\big(x,t;\, s_\theta\big),$$ where $x$ is the state being denoised, $t$ runs over the diffusion time, and $f$ is built from the frozen score network $s_\theta$ (the network estimating $\nabla_x \log p_t(x)$, the gradient of the log-density). The solver is a fixed Euler scheme on a grid of $K = \text{NFE}$ points; the report normalization and deployment NFE are all held fixed. A terminal report is a functional $R(x_T)$ of the final state — a covariance, a tail quantile, a drawdown proxy.

The key equations. At each grid interval TAPES estimates the signed local truncation defect $\delta_k$ by a Richardson comparison (coarse step vs. two half-steps). It then transports each defect to the terminal report through the linearized remaining flow and a terminal-report adjoint $\lambda_k$ (the sensitivity of $R$ to a perturbation at step $k$, propagated backward). The paper's central identity is that the first-order error in the reported quantity is the transported signed sum $$\Delta R \;\approx\; \sum_{k} \lambda_k^{\top}\, \delta_k,$$ and TAPES chooses the grid to minimize its squared norm $$\min_{\text{grid}} \; \Big\| \sum_k \lambda_k^{\top}\,\delta_k \Big\|^2$$ before a capped direct refinement. Here $\delta_k$ is the local defect vector at step $k$ and $\lambda_k$ the adjoint weight; the key point is the signed sum can exhibit cancellation that a diagonal (per-step) criterion ignores.

The method. The adjoint is computed by backward propagation through the discrete flow (reverse-mode sensitivity), defects by Richardson extrapolation, and the grid by minimizing the transported-error objective — an offline optimization that changes only the time-grid, not the network, solver, or NFE. Under an "aligned-defect" model they quantify analytically how much a diagonal criterion loses by ignoring sign cancellation.

Results with numbers. Across 6 budgets × datasets × reports (24 rows in the five-pipeline panel) TAPES attains the lowest design- and audit-report error in every row. Cross-budget mean relative audit-error reductions are about 10–11% on the Regime-SV simulation and about 1% on the Mag7 contexts, with Bonferroni intervals excluding zero; winners persist under a doubled-resolution reference, and a small Heun-solver probe preserves the TAPES-first ordering.

Jargon Decoded

  • Diffusion model — a generative model that creates samples by gradually denoising, reversing a noising process.
  • Probability-flow ODE — the deterministic differential equation whose solution trajectories match a diffusion model's distribution.
  • Score network — the neural network estimating the gradient of the log-density that drives the denoising dynamics.
  • NFE (number of function evaluations) — how many times the network is called per sample; the compute budget and the source of grid error.
  • Richardson extrapolation — estimating a numerical method's error by comparing a coarse step to finer sub-steps.
  • Adjoint method — backward-propagating sensitivities to measure how a final output responds to perturbations at each earlier step.
  • Terminal report — the specific end-of-solve statistic (covariance, tail, drawdown) whose accuracy is being optimized.

Honest Take

The gains are report-specific and modest where it may matter most: ~1% on the realistic Mag7 contexts versus ~10% on the synthetic regime-switching data, so the payoff depends heavily on how far your deployment looks like the stylized simulation. It is a first-order (linearized) error theory, so it can mislead when step-errors are large or the report is highly nonlinear, and it assumes you have one fixed report in mind — re-optimizing per statistic adds offline cost. Still, because it touches nothing but the grid, it is a low-risk drop-in for anyone already running few-step financial diffusion samplers.
