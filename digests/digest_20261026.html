1. On the continuity of conditional distributions and stratification for derivative pricing

Aleksandar Arandjelovic, Patrick Cheridito
q-fin.CP · 2026-10-23 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

A neural network learns the single best "label" for sorting random market scenarios into buckets, so that pricing a fiendishly complex derivative by simulation gets far more accurate for the same amount of computing.

Plain English

The problem. Banks constantly need to put a price on complicated financial contracts — things whose payoff depends on the entire path a price takes, not just where it ends up. There's usually no formula, so the standard trick is Monte Carlo: simulate tens of thousands of possible future paths of interest rates or stock prices, compute what the contract pays on each one, and average. It's like estimating your average commute by driving on many random days and averaging the times. The catch is noise. Random samples scatter, so to pin the average down tightly you need a lot of them — and each simulation costs real computer time and money.

The idea. There's an old statistics trick to cut the noise called stratification. Instead of sampling blindly, you first sort your scenarios into groups ("strata") and sample each group in proportion. Think of a pollster who, rather than phoning random people, deliberately samples the right fraction of each age bracket and region — the estimate steadies because you're no longer at the mercy of a lopsided random draw. The whole method lives or dies on one choice: what label do you sort by? You want a single summary number — the "stratification factor" — that captures most of what makes the contract's payoff swing around. In a messy, high-dimensional model, nobody knows what that number should be. The authors' move is to stop guessing and let a neural network — a flexible, trainable function — learn the best sorting label straight from the model.

What they found. The learned labels beat every hand-crafted benchmark they tried, on two genuinely hard real-world contracts: an interest-rate "range accrual" note and a "worst-of autocallable" tied to several assets at once. Just as important, they prove the method rests on solid ground — that approximating the ideal label with a network is mathematically safe, with sharp caveats about when it is not.

The Math

The setup. We want the price of a claim, which is an expected payoff $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$, where $X$ is the (possibly path-valued) random state of a continuous-time model and $g$ is the payoff. A stratification factor is a real-valued function $\xi = f(X)$. We partition its range into $K$ strata $A_1,\dots,A_K$ and, under proportional allocation, sample each stratum with its natural probability $p_k = \mathbb{P}(\xi \in A_k)$.

The key equation. Everything hinges on the law of total variance: $$\mathrm{Var}\big(g(X)\big) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}(g(X)\mid \xi)\big]}_{\text{within-strata, the noise you keep}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid \xi]\big)}_{\text{between-strata, the noise you remove}}.$$ Here $g(X)$ is the random payoff, $\xi=f(X)$ the stratification factor, $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\mid\xi]$ the conditional expectation given that factor, and $\mathrm{Var}(\cdot\mid\xi)$ the conditional variance. With fine proportional stratification the estimator's variance shrinks toward $\tfrac1N\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(g\mid\xi)]$ for $N$ samples. So the best factor is the one that maximizes the second term — the variance it can explain — and the ideal is $\xi \propto \mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid X]$ projected onto one dimension.

The method. Since that ideal is unknown, parametrize $f=f_\theta$ as a neural network and train $\theta$ to maximize the explained variance $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[g\mid f_\theta(X)])$. The theoretical heart of the paper is a continuity question: the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid f(X)]$ is itself a function of the factor $f$, and if we only have an approximation $f_n\to f$, we need the conditional expectations (and more generally the conditional distributions) to converge too. They prove this holds under suitable conditions — and give explicit counterexamples where it fails. Combined with the universal-approximation (density) property of neural networks, this yields a universal approximation theorem for admissible stratification factors: networks can get arbitrarily close to the best possible factor.

Results with numbers. On a constant-maturity-swap range accrual in a two-factor Gaussian short-rate model, and a worst-of autocallable in a Wishart stochastic-covariance model, the learned neural factors delivered lower estimator variance than all considered benchmarks — the headline being that a trained one-dimensional label can out-stratify heuristics designed by hand.

Jargon Decoded

  • Monte Carlo pricing — estimating a contract's value by averaging its payoff over many simulated random futures.
  • Stratification — cutting the sample space into groups and sampling each in proportion, to shrink the scatter of the average.
  • Stratification factor — the one-dimensional summary number you sort scenarios by; its quality sets how much variance you remove.
  • Conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[g\mid\xi]$ — the average payoff among all scenarios sharing the same value of the factor $\xi$.
  • Universal approximation theorem — the guarantee that neural networks can approximate essentially any reasonable function as closely as you like.
  • Autocallable / range accrual — structured notes whose payoff depends on the whole path of rates or several assets, not just the final level.

Honest Take

The guarantees are asymptotic ("a network can approximate the ideal factor"), which says nothing about how much training data or compute you need to get there, and the counterexamples warn that the continuity it leans on genuinely can break. Training the factor adds an offline cost that only pays off if you reprice the same contract many times. Still, for a desk that revalues the same exotic book daily, a learned, model-agnostic variance reducer with a proof behind it is a real and usable win.

Bottom Line

Stop hand-picking how to bucket your simulations and let a network learn it — the math says that's safe, and the experiments say it's better.

2. Optimal Size in Market Making under State-Dependent Hawkes Process

Jonathan Chávez Casillas, Li Gan
q-fin.TR · 2026-10-22 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

A market maker's ideal order size can be read straight off a formula that links how much volume you actually execute to how deep you post your quotes and to a self-exciting model of how orders arrive in bursts.

Plain English

The problem. A market maker is a trader who continuously offers to both buy (a bit below the current price) and sell (a bit above it), earning the small gap — the spread — on each round trip. The danger is inventory: if you keep buying and the price falls, you're stuck holding a loss. So the market maker faces a constant tension — post aggressively to capture more trades, or hold back to control inventory. A concrete everyday version: a used-car dealer decides how keenly to bid for cars; bid high and you fill your lot fast but risk a glut you can't sell. The quantitative question is: given where you place your quotes, how much volume will actually trade against you, and what's the optimal size to offer?

The idea. Real order flow isn't a steady drizzle — it comes in bursts. One trade tends to trigger more, like aftershocks following an earthquake. The authors model arrivals with a Hawkes process (a "self-exciting" clock where each event briefly raises the chance of the next) and make it state-dependent, so the burst rate reacts to the current state of the order book. They then write executed volume as a product of three intuitive pieces: a typical order size, a term that falls off as you quote further from the mid-price (people trade less with you when your price is worse), and a flexible "burstiness" multiplier built from a sum of decaying exponentials (a Prony series) driven by the running count of market orders.

What they found. Feeding this into a utility-maximizing size choice and calibrating it to real limit-order-book data, their selected policy earns higher end-of-day utility than a baseline while still respecting a hard cap on inventory — and they can cleanly separate how much of the gain came from trading revenue versus from better inventory control.

The Math

The setup. Orders arrive via a state-dependent Hawkes process with conditional intensity $$\lambda(t) = \mu\big(s(t)\big) + \sum_{t_i < t} \phi\big(t - t_i\big),$$ where $\lambda(t)$ is the instantaneous arrival rate, $\mu(s(t))$ a baseline depending on the current book state $s(t)$, the sum runs over past event times $t_i$, and $\phi$ is the excitation kernel — each past order lifts the current rate, producing clustering.

The key equation. Executed volume is modeled as a product, $$V(\delta, N) = \bar{q}\cdot e^{-\beta \delta}\cdot M(\delta, N),$$ where $V$ is executed volume, $\delta$ the quote depth (distance from the mid-price), $\bar q$ the average order size, $e^{-\beta\delta}$ an exponential demand function with sensitivity $\beta>0$ (deeper quotes trade less), $N$ the market-order count generated by the Hawkes model, and $M(\delta, N)=\sum_{j} a_j\,e^{-b_j\,x}$ a Prony-series multiplier — a finite sum of exponentials with weights $a_j$ and decay rates $b_j$. The paper proves an approximation result that finite Prony sums can represent the relevant response on a compact state domain, which is what makes this representation legitimate rather than arbitrary.

The method. With volume pinned down as a function of placement, the market maker chooses quote levels to maximize terminal utility subject to an inventory bound, and the model is calibrated to limit-order-book data (fitting the demand sensitivity and the Hawkes intensity). Because $V$ is closed-form in the placement levels, it plugs into alternative quoting rules without re-deriving everything.

Results with numbers. In retrospective evaluations conditioned on realized order-flow paths, the chosen calibration achieved higher terminal utility than a baseline quoting policy while respecting the prescribed inventory bound, and the authors decompose the performance to show how much came from trading revenue versus inventory control.

Jargon Decoded

  • Market making — continuously quoting both a buy and a sell price and earning the spread between them.
  • Inventory risk — the danger of being left holding a position that moves against you.
  • Hawkes process — a point process where each event temporarily raises the rate of future events, producing clustered "bursty" arrivals.
  • State-dependent intensity — letting that arrival rate also depend on the current state of the order book.
  • Prony series — a finite sum of decaying exponentials used as a flexible, tractable function approximator.
  • Quote depth — how far from the mid-price you post your order; deeper means safer but less likely to fill.

Honest Take

The evaluations are retrospective — replayed against order-flow paths that already happened — so they don't prove the policy survives its own market impact once it is actually trading and changing the flow. Calibrating a state-dependent Hawkes model is delicate and can overfit. But the closed-form volume map is genuinely modular: a desk can bolt it onto quoting rules it already trusts, which lowers the bar to trying it.

Bottom Line

Model the bursts honestly and your optimal quote size falls out of a formula you can drop into whatever quoting engine you already run.

3. Mimicking Volatilities under Marginal Constraints via $q_x$-Bass Martingales: A Parallel Computing Approach

Antonio Marini
q-fin.MF · 2026-10-22 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

A method for building a continuous-time price model that exactly reproduces today's option prices while staying as close as possible to a volatility you already trust — and does so without ever needing the full probability distributions.

Plain English

The problem. Option prices quoted in the market quietly encode what traders think a stock might do. At each expiry date, the spread of option prices pins down the market's implied probability distribution for the stock on that date — these are the "marginals." A desk wants one coherent, continuous-time model whose randomness reproduces all of those snapshot distributions simultaneously, so it can price and hedge exotic contracts consistently. The complication: there are infinitely many models that match the snapshots, and most of them behave wildly between the dates. You also usually have a reference model you trust — say a well-worn local-volatility surface — and you'd like to match the market prices while deviating from that trusted volatility as little as possible.

The idea. This is optimal transport with a twist. Ordinary optimal transport asks for the cheapest way to move one pile of sand into another. Here the "piles" are the option-implied distributions at successive dates, and the "cost" penalizes straying from the reference volatility — but with a crucial constraint: the price process must be a martingale (a mathematically fair game, no free drift), because that's what no-arbitrage demands. The authors use a clever construction called Bass martingales (here a variant they call $q_x$-Bass) that builds such a process by passing a Brownian motion through a cleverly chosen map, so the marginals come out right by construction. They then chop time into steps, solve the easier discrete problems, and prove the stitched-together solutions converge to the true continuous answer.

What they found. Their discrete schemes converge to genuinely optimal continuous-time models, and — the practical payoff — the numerical methods don't require the full marginal distributions at the calibration maturities, only more modest ingredients, which is what makes the whole thing computable in parallel at realistic scale.

The Math

The setup. Work on a filtered probability space with a price process $(S_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$ required to be a martingale, $\mathbb{E}[S_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s]=S_s$ for $s\le t$, and to match prescribed marginals $S_t\sim\mu_t$ recovered from option prices (via Breeden–Litzenberger). Let $\sigma_t$ denote the model's local volatility and $\sigma_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$ that of a reference model.

The key equation. The calibration is a dynamic martingale optimal transport problem, $$\inf_{(S_t)}\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_0^T c\big(\sigma_t,\ \sigma_t^{\mathrm{ref}}\big)\,dt\right]\quad\text{subject to}\quad S_t\sim\mu_t,\ \ (S_t)\ \text{a martingale},$$ where the infimum is over admissible martingales, $c(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a cost penalizing deviation of the model volatility $\sigma_t$ from the reference $\sigma_t^{\mathrm{ref}}$, and $\mu_t$ are the target marginals. A Bass martingale has the structural form $S_t=\mathbb{E}[F(W_T)\mid W_t]$ for a Brownian motion $W$ and a suitable map $F$, which automatically produces the desired terminal marginal; the $q_x$-variant extends this to the multi-maturity, volatility-penalized setting.

The method. Approximate the continuous problem by a sequence of discrete-time problems over the $q_x$-Bass family, solve those numerically (exploiting that full marginals aren't needed), and prove that the continuous-time interpolations of the discrete optimizers are asymptotically optimal — i.e., as the time grid refines, the discrete solutions converge to a true optimizer of the continuous problem. The computation parallelizes across the discrete sub-problems.

Results with numbers. The paper's contributions are structural rather than a horse-race table: the asymptotic-optimality theorem for the discrete-to-continuous interpolations, and numerical schemes that sidestep the need for full marginal distributions at calibration maturities.

Jargon Decoded

  • Marginal distribution — the probability distribution of the asset price at a single future date, read off option prices.
  • Martingale — a process with no predictable drift; required by no-arbitrage for a discounted price.
  • Martingale optimal transport (MOT) — optimal transport constrained so the coupling is a martingale with prescribed marginals.
  • Bass martingale — a martingale built as a conditioned function of Brownian motion that hits a target marginal by construction.
  • Calibration — tuning a model so its prices match the market's quoted option prices.
  • Breeden–Litzenberger — the fact that the second derivative of option price in strike gives the implied density.

Honest Take

This is a theory-and-method paper — the headline is an asymptotic-optimality proof and a computational scheme, not a demonstration that it reprices a live exotic book better than incumbents. Whether the volatility-deviation penalty produces hedges traders actually like, and how it behaves when the market marginals are noisy or arbitrageable, remains to be shown. But "match the prices, stay near a trusted vol, and never need the full distribution" is exactly the shape of problem calibration desks fight with daily.

Bottom Line

Calibrate to the whole option surface while hugging a volatility you trust — provably, in discrete steps, without ever writing down the full distributions.

4. Deep catastrophic-risk-aware reinforcement learning

Carlos Pérez Octavio Mendoza, Frédéric Godin
cs.LG · 2026-10-19 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

By borrowing the statistics of rare extremes, a hedging AI can learn to guard against financial disasters even when it has almost no examples of them to learn from.

Plain English

The problem. Suppose you train an AI to hedge a portfolio — to place trades that cancel out risk. You'd like it to care especially about catastrophes: the worst 1-in-1000 days. A standard way to measure that is Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), the average loss on your very worst outcomes. But here's the trap: at a 99.9% confidence level, almost none of your simulated scenarios land in that extreme tail. With only a handful of disaster examples, the AI's estimate of tail risk is pure noise, and the "learning signal" it needs to improve is too faint to follow. It's like trying to learn how to survive once-a-century floods from a dataset with one flood in it.

The idea. Statisticians have a whole theory for exactly this — Extreme Value Theory (EVT) — which says that, under broad conditions, the shape of the far tail is universal: losses that exceed a high threshold follow a particular curve (a generalized Pareto distribution). So instead of counting the few disasters you happened to see, you fit that universal tail shape to the merely-bad events (which are plentiful) and extrapolate out to the catastrophic ones. The authors turn this into a smooth, differentiable training objective — crucial, because modern AI learns by nudging millions of parameters in the direction that improves the objective, which requires that objective to have a usable gradient.

What they found. They first checked the method on a toy problem where the right answer is known, then on deep hedging with rare extreme market moves. Under normal conditions, the EVT-trained policy and a plain CVaR-trained policy behave about the same — but when you stress-test them on extreme scenarios, the EVT-trained policy takes lower hedging losses, and this held across the training sample sizes they tried. In short: when disaster data is scarce, EVT buys you a more robust policy.

The Math

The setup. Let $L$ be the loss of a policy $\pi_\theta$ (a neural network with parameters $\theta$). Value-at-Risk at level $\alpha$ is the $\alpha$-quantile $\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)$, and Conditional Value-at-Risk is the expected loss beyond it: $$\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(L)=\mathbb{E}\big[L \,\big|\, L\ge \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\big].$$ Here $\alpha$ is the confidence level (e.g. 0.999), $L$ the random loss, and $\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$ its $\alpha$-quantile; CVaR averages the tail above that quantile.

The key equation. EVT's peaks-over-threshold representation says exceedances over a high threshold $u$ are approximately generalized-Pareto: $$\mathbb{P}\big(L-u \le y \,\big|\, L>u\big)\ \approx\ 1-\Big(1+\tfrac{\xi y}{\beta}\Big)^{-1/\xi},$$ where $u$ is the threshold, $y\ge 0$ the exceedance size, $\xi$ the tail (shape) index governing how heavy the tail is, and $\beta>0$ a scale parameter. Given $\xi,\beta$, CVaR beyond a high level has a closed form in these parameters, so estimating the tail replaces counting rare samples.

The method. Estimate $\hat\xi,\hat\beta$ by method of moments (matching sample moments of the exceedances), yielding a differentiable tail-risk objective. The key technical point: conditional on the selected threshold $u$, the finite-sample gradient of this objective can be computed directly through automatic differentiation, so it slots straight into standard deep-RL training to optimize the neural policy $\pi_\theta$.

Results with numbers. Validated first on a controlled problem with a known optimal solution. In a deep-hedging task with rare extreme moves, EVT- and empirical-CVaR-trained policies hedged similarly under normal conditions, while the EVT-trained policies showed lower hedging losses under extreme-scenario evaluation, consistently across the training sample sizes considered.

Jargon Decoded

  • Reinforcement learning (RL) — training an agent to choose actions by rewarding good outcomes over many simulated episodes.
  • Value-at-Risk (VaR) — a loss level you expect to exceed only with small probability $1-\alpha$.
  • Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) — the average loss given you're already in that bad tail; a coherent, tail-focused risk measure.
  • Extreme Value Theory (EVT) — the mathematics of rare extremes, which says tails converge to a universal (generalized Pareto / extreme-value) shape.
  • Peaks-over-threshold — modeling only the exceedances above a high threshold with a generalized Pareto distribution.
  • Deep hedging — using a neural network to learn a hedging strategy directly from simulated market scenarios.

Honest Take

EVT's magic rests on the threshold and the assumption that the tail really is generalized-Pareto; a badly chosen threshold or a tail that doesn't fit the theory can quietly mislead, and the gradient is only computed conditional on that threshold. The gains showed up in simulated extreme scenarios, not live markets with structural breaks the model never saw. But as a drop-in, differentiable way to make a hedging network take rare disasters seriously, it's a sensible and honest contribution.

Bottom Line

When catastrophes are too rare to learn from directly, fit the universal shape of the tail and train against that instead.

5. Probability-Weighting Functions for Normal Tempered-Stable Laws: Orthogonal Channels and Information-Theoretic Indices

Akash Deep, Bhathiya Divelgama, A. Alexandre Trindade, W. Brent Lindquist, Svetlozar T. Rachev, Frank J. Fabozzi
q-fin.MF · 2026-10-19 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

Rather than assuming a formula for how people mentally distort probabilities, derive that distortion directly from how the return distribution itself shifts — and three different kinds of shift leave three cleanly distinguishable fingerprints.

Plain English

The problem. People don't treat probabilities at face value. Behavioral finance has long known we overweight tiny chances (why lottery tickets and crash insurance sell) and underweight near-certainties. Economists capture this with a probability-weighting function (PWF) that bends objective probabilities into the "decision weights" people actually act on. The usual approach is to pick a convenient curve with a couple of knobs and fit it. But that bakes the shape in by assumption, and it doesn't tell you why the distortion looks the way it does or what changed in the world to produce it.

The idea. The authors flip it around. Suppose your beliefs about returns shift from a "prior" distribution to a "posterior" one. The weighting function should then be whatever transformation carries one into the other: line the two distributions up by their percentiles. Concretely, take an objective probability $p$, find the return at that prior percentile, then ask what posterior probability sits below it. They let both distributions belong to a rich, fat-tailed family (normal tempered-stable, a realistic model for financial returns) and let the posterior differ from the prior along exactly one of three "channels" at a time — a change in scale (roughly, volatility), in skewness (asymmetry), or in tail thickness — each isolated with the overall variance held fixed. The beautiful result is that each channel distorts probabilities with its own signature.

What they found. The fingerprints are strikingly clean: a scale change makes the distortion $w(p)-p$ cross zero once, a skewness change makes it cross twice, and a tail-thickness change makes it cross three times. On numbers calibrated to real S&P 500 ETF returns from 2015–2025, these channels produced probability distortions of about 2 to 7 percentage points — small-sounding, but exactly the magnitudes that move real decisions about insurance and tail bets.

The Math

The setup. With prior and posterior return distributions $F_{\mathrm{prior}}$ and $F_{\mathrm{post}}$, define the probability-weighting function directly as $$w(p) = F_{\mathrm{post}}\big(F_{\mathrm{prior}}^{-1}(p)\big),$$ where $p\in(0,1)$ is an objective probability, $F_{\mathrm{prior}}^{-1}(p)$ the prior's $p$-quantile (the return at that percentile), and $F_{\mathrm{post}}$ the posterior CDF evaluated there. Both $F_{\mathrm{prior}},F_{\mathrm{post}}$ are normal tempered-stable (NTS) laws — distributions obtained by running a Brownian motion on a tempered-stable "business clock," giving separate control of scale, skewness, and tail index while keeping finite moments.

The key equations. The posterior deviates from a symmetric benchmark (excess kurtosis 3) along one channel at a time, variance fixed. The reported sensitivities: a 40% change in scale shifts cumulative probabilities by up to 7–8 percentage points; a skewness of $\pm 0.5$ by about 2 percentage points; a tail-index change of $\pm 0.3$ by about 2 percentage points. The signatures are counted by the sign changes of the deviation $w(p)-p$: once (scale), twice (skewness), three times (tail). To summarize distortions without the blow-up of the classical ratio $(w(p)-p)/[p(1-p)]$ near the endpoints, they introduce three information-theoretic indices — a Fisher-normalized logit shift, a signed Jensen–Shannon divergence, and a log-odds elasticity — each a single number grading the strength and direction of the distortion.

The method. Construction is analytic in the NTS parameters: fix a symmetric benchmark, perturb one channel with variance held constant, and read off $w(p)=F_{\mathrm{post}}(F_{\mathrm{prior}}^{-1}(p))$. The NTS parameters are then calibrated to S&P 500 ETF daily returns over 2015–2025, and the same channel analysis is applied to the fitted distributions. Fully reproducible code is provided.

Results with numbers. On the calibrated S&P 500 data the three channels generated distortions of 2 to 7 percentage points, matching the controlled-benchmark magnitudes, with the one/two/three sign-change signatures intact.

Jargon Decoded

  • Probability-weighting function (PWF) — the map from objective probabilities to the decision weights people actually use.
  • Normal tempered-stable (NTS) law — a flexible fat-tailed distribution built by time-changing Brownian motion, with separate dials for scale, skew, and tail.
  • Tail index — a parameter controlling how heavy the extreme tails are (how likely big moves are).
  • Excess kurtosis — a measure of how fat-tailed a distribution is relative to the normal (whose excess kurtosis is 0).
  • Jensen–Shannon divergence — a symmetric, bounded information-theoretic measure of how different two distributions are.
  • Log-odds elasticity — how responsive the odds $w/(1-w)$ are to the underlying odds $p/(1-p)$, in percentage terms.

Honest Take

This is a modeling-and-measurement paper, not evidence about real human choices — the "weighting" is derived from a distributional shift, so it only equals behavioral probability weighting if people's belief updates actually move along these NTS channels, which the paper doesn't test against choice data. The three-channel decomposition is elegant but presumes the NTS family captures the relevant moves. Where it's genuinely useful is diagnostic: given two fitted return distributions, the sign-change signature tells you which feature changed, with reproducible code to back it.

Bottom Line

Don't assume the shape of probability distortion — derive it from how the return distribution moved, and count the zero-crossings to see exactly what changed.
