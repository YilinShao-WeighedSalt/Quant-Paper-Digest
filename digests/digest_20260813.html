1. Optimal Pricing and Hedging of SOFR Derivatives

Teemu Pennanen, Waleed Taoum
q-fin.PR · 2026-08-11 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

It prices illiquid interest-rate derivatives by asking "what price leaves a trader exactly as well off as walking away?" instead of pretending the market is complete enough to hedge perfectly.

Plain English

The problem. SOFR — the Secured Overnight Financing Rate — is the benchmark interest rate that US dollar contracts now reference after LIBOR was retired. Thousands of derivatives (swaps, futures, options) pay off based on it. But most trade thinly: you can't always find a counterparty, and you certainly can't buy a perfect offsetting hedge for every exotic over-the-counter (OTC) contract. Classic derivative pricing — the risk-neutral, Black–Scholes tradition — quietly assumes you can replicate any payoff by continuously trading liquid instruments, so the price is unique and hedging is exact. In an illiquid, incomplete market that assumption is a fiction, and it hides the fact that whoever holds the derivative is stuck carrying real, unhedgeable risk. Think of insuring a one-of-a-kind vintage car: there's no liquid market of identical cars to hedge against, so you price the policy by your own appetite for risk and what's already in your garage.

The idea. The paper uses indifference pricing. The price of a derivative is defined as the amount of cash that makes you genuinely indifferent between two worlds: one where you take on the derivative (plus that cash) and then trade optimally, and one where you never touched it. Your existing positions, your views about the future, and how much you dislike risk all feed into the number — the risk attitude encoded by a convex risk measure, a mathematically coherent scoring rule that assigns a "badness" number to any uncertain payoff and rewards diversification. Because perfect hedging is impossible, the model doesn't stop at a price: it hands you the best available hedge and an explicit description of the leftover hedging error and how risky that leftover is.

What they found. Feeding in hundreds of CME-listed SOFR derivatives as hedging building blocks, the authors price and hedge un-replicable OTC SOFR contracts, with indifference prices computed in under a minute on an ordinary PC. The optimal hedges turn out to be sparse — they lean on only a handful of instruments — yet still approximate the target payoffs well. Compared to the classical approach, which either fails outright in an incomplete market or gives a hedge whose error you can't even quantify, this delivers a price, a hedge, and an honest error bar together.

The Math

The setup. Work on a probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$. The agent has a baseline random wealth/position $X$ and can trade a set of liquid hedging instruments with payoffs $H_i$ at observed prices $p_i$, choosing quantities $\theta_i$. Risk preferences are captured by a convex risk measure $\rho$ acting on random payoffs.

The key equations. A convex risk measure satisfies monotonicity, cash-invariance $\rho(Y+m)=\rho(Y)-m$, and convexity, and admits the dual representation $$\rho(Y)=\sup_{\mathbb{Q}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[-Y]-\alpha(\mathbb{Q})\big\},$$ where $\mathbb{Q}$ ranges over probability measures and $\alpha(\mathbb{Q})$ is a penalty scoring how implausible scenario $\mathbb{Q}$ is. The (buyer's) indifference price $\pi(C)$ of a claim paying $C$ is defined implicitly by $$\inf_{\theta}\;\rho\!\Big(X+C-\pi(C)+\textstyle\sum_i \theta_i (H_i-p_i)\Big)\;=\;\inf_{\theta}\;\rho\!\Big(X+\textstyle\sum_i \theta_i (H_i-p_i)\Big).$$ Here $C$ is the derivative's payoff, $\pi(C)$ the price being solved for, $\theta_i$ the hedge quantity in instrument $i$, and $H_i-p_i$ its net (payoff-minus-price) return. Left side = optimally-hedged risk with the claim and cash paid; right side = optimally-hedged risk without it. The residual position $R=C+\sum_i\theta_i^\star(H_i-p_i)$ (at the optimal $\theta^\star$) is the explicit hedging error, and $\rho(R)$ measures its risk.

The method. Both sides are convex minimizations, so pricing and hedging reduce to a convex optimization problem solved numerically — and convex duality is what turns the abstract $\rho$ into a tractable "worst-case over scenarios" program. That structure is why hundreds of hedging instruments can be handled in under a minute.

Results with numbers. Using hundreds of CME SOFR instruments to hedge OTC SOFR derivatives: sub-minute computation on a regular PC; sparse optimal portfolios that nonetheless track the payoffs closely; and, unlike risk-neutral pricing, a quantified hedging error and its associated risk delivered alongside every price.

Jargon Decoded

  • SOFR — the benchmark overnight US-dollar borrowing rate that replaced LIBOR, referenced by huge volumes of derivatives.
  • indifference price — the cash amount that leaves a trader equally well off whether or not they take the position.
  • convex risk measure — a coherent function scoring the riskiness of a random payoff, rewarding diversification.
  • incomplete market — one where not every payoff can be perfectly replicated by trading the available assets.
  • convex duality — rewriting an optimization as an equivalent worst-case-scenario problem, which makes it computable.
  • hedging error — the leftover random payoff you're still exposed to after hedging as best you can.

Honest Take

Indifference prices are inherently subjective: they depend on the agent's book, views, and chosen risk measure, so two desks legitimately compute two different prices — there is no single "the" price, and the choice of $\rho$ is where real model risk hides. But for genuinely illiquid OTC SOFR exposures, explicitly quantifying the unavoidable hedging error is far more honest than the risk-neutral pretense that a perfect price exists, and the sub-minute speed makes it usable on a desk.

Bottom Line

When you can't hedge perfectly, stop pretending you can — price the contract by how much risk you're willing to keep.

2. Multi-Credit Calibration via Elastically Stopped Lévy Processes

Graeme Baker, Agostino Capponi
q-fin.MF · 2026-08-10 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

A credit model where a company defaults the moment its hidden "distress level" first pokes above a random threshold — surprising like real defaults, yet built from interpretable structure, and able to make many firms fail together through one shared shock.

Plain English

The problem. A credit default swap (CDS) is insurance against a company defaulting: you pay a periodic fee, and if the firm goes under, you get paid. An index tranche bundles many firms' default risk and slices it by seniority — the junior slice absorbs the first losses, the senior slice only the catastrophic tail. To price these you need a model that (1) fits the quotes actually seen in the market, (2) captures that defaults cluster — in 2008 firms toppled together — and (3) computes fast. Two classic families each fail one test. Structural models (a firm defaults when its asset value hits a barrier) are beautifully interpretable but make defaults predictable — you'd see the barrier approaching — which badly mis-prices near-term risk. Reduced-form models fit prices well but treat default as a black-box arrival rate with no story.

The idea. This paper fuses the two. Each firm carries a latent "distress" process that only ever jumps upward (a spectrally positive Lévy process). The firm defaults when the running maximum of its distress first crosses a threshold — but the threshold is itself random (an independent exponential draw), like a tripwire set at a height you can't see. Because the tripwire height is random, default arrives as a genuine surprise (a "totally inaccessible" time) even though the mechanism is fully structural. Then, to make firms default together, the authors add a single shared jump factor — one common shock that nudges every firm's distress up at the same instant — giving contagion and even simultaneous defaults from just two extra parameters. Picture many dams, each with a hidden overflow line drawn at a secret height; usually they rise independently, but one shared flood can push several over their lines at once.

What they found. On daily CDX North American High-Yield and Investment-Grade panels, the model posts the lowest out-of-sample errors in a six-model contest, and — crucially — reproduces the inverted CDS spread curves of firms sliding into default, a shape a plain Lévy subordinator model provably cannot produce. At the index level, the two-parameter dependence structure closes 73%–89% of the tranche-pricing gap that independent firms leave open, rising to 95% once re-marked to tranche quotes. It beats both the standard single-factor Gaussian copula and the Duffie–Gârleanu affine-intensity benchmark on both indices.

The Math

The setup. Each firm's latent distress is a spectrally positive Lévy process $X_t$ (jumps upward, stationary independent increments). Let its running supremum be $\bar{X}_t=\sup_{0\le s\le t}X_s$. The default time is $$\tau=\inf\{t\ge 0:\bar{X}_t\ge E\},\qquad E\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\ \text{independent of }X.$$ Here $E$ is the random barrier and $\lambda$ its rate. Conditioning on $X$, this is a Cox construction, and $\tau$ is totally inaccessible.

The key equations. The survival probability becomes a Laplace transform of the running maximum: $$\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\lambda \bar{X}_t}\right].$$ The paper's engine is a single-name result: under phase-type jumps, the Laplace transform (in time) of the default probability has a finite partial-fraction formula, obtained via the Wiener–Hopf factorization, which splits the Laplace exponent of $X$ into pieces governing its running maximum and running minimum. For the multi-credit model, a common compound-Poisson jump $J_t$ is added to every firm's driver, $X^{(i)}_t = Y^{(i)}_t + J_t$, so that a jump in $J$ can trip several barriers simultaneously.

The method. Single-name default probabilities come in closed form from Wiener–Hopf plus partial fractions; the multi-name index and tranches are priced by an exact Wiener–Hopf Monte Carlo scheme (Monte Carlo with no time-discretization bias in the supremum). Calibration fits the driver parameters to CDS and tranche quotes.

Results with numbers. Lowest out-of-sample error among six models on CDX HY and IG; reproduces inverted spread curves (subordinator models cannot); dependence closes 73%–89% (up to 95% re-marked) of the tranche gap; dominates Gaussian copula and Duffie–Gârleanu affine intensity on both indices.

Jargon Decoded

  • credit default swap (CDS) — a contract that pays out if a specific company defaults, i.e. default insurance.
  • index tranche — a slice of the pooled default losses of many firms, absorbing losses within a fixed seniority range.
  • Lévy process — a stochastic process with independent, stationary increments, allowing jumps.
  • spectrally positive — a jump process whose jumps are all upward.
  • running supremum — the highest value the process has reached up to time $t$.
  • totally inaccessible default time — a default that arrives as a true surprise, not foreseeable an instant before.
  • Wiener–Hopf factorization — a technique splitting a process into its running-max and running-min parts, enabling closed-form transforms.

Honest Take

The tractability leans on phase-type jumps and a single common factor, so richer contagion — sector-specific clusters, chains of defaults — may be under-modeled, and the last slice of tranche accuracy needs re-marking to quotes rather than emerging purely from the drivers. Wiener–Hopf Monte Carlo is exact but still Monte Carlo, heavier than a copula for a desk wanting instant re-pricing. Within those limits, though, it's a rare model that keeps structural interpretability while matching reduced-form fit.

Bottom Line

Default as the surprise crossing of an invisible, randomly-set line — structural interpretability with reduced-form realism, and contagion from a single shared shock.

3. High-Order Expansions of the Optimizer Map via Bell Polynomials

Oleksii Mostovyi, Thaleia Zariphopoulou
q-fin.MF · 2026-08-09 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

It proves that when you nudge an investor's risk preferences, their optimal portfolio changes smoothly and predictably — expressible as a convergent power series whose coefficients come from a tidy combinatorial formula.

Plain English

The problem. Every optimal-investment recipe depends on the investor's utility function — the mathematical description of how they trade reward against risk. But utility is never known exactly; it's assumed, estimated, or elicited, and it's uncertain. So a natural worry: if you got the utility slightly wrong, how far off is the "optimal" portfolio? If a tiny misjudgment of someone's risk appetite flips their strategy from cautious to aggressive, the optimization is fragile and untrustworthy. Everyday version: a cake recipe tuned for "medium sweetness." If your actual taste is a touch sweeter, does the ideal amount of sugar shift a little — or does the whole recipe fall apart? You want a smooth dial, not a cliff edge.

The idea. The authors restrict attention to a well-behaved family of preferences called CMIM (completely monotonic inverse marginal) utilities — a class that happens to contain the workhorses of finance, power and exponential utilities. These utilities have a special Bernstein representation: their inverse marginal can be written as a continuous blend of simple decaying exponentials. That blend hides a linear structure inside the dual optimization problem. So when you perturb the investor's preferences, the value of the problem responds in an affine (straight-line-like) way, and the entire dependence on preferences collapses onto a single scalar "budget" equation. From there they prove the crucial control variable is analytic in the perturbation — meaning it equals its own Taylor series — and they write down that series to any order, with coefficients organized by Bell polynomials, the combinatorial bookkeeping device that handles the higher derivatives of composed functions.

What they found. They obtain convergent analytic expansions, of arbitrary order, for both the value function and the optimal terminal wealth, with explicit recursive formulas. This is a structural guarantee rather than a horse-race with baselines: it certifies that optimal investment depends smoothly on preferences and gives a directly computable way to get first-, second-, and higher-order sensitivities — a rigorous foundation for "how wrong can I be, and does it matter?"

The Math

The setup. Consider a market that is stochastically dominant and a utility $U$ in the CMIM class, so its inverse marginal $I=(U')^{-1}$ is completely monotone. By Bernstein's theorem it has the representation $$I(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-ys}\,d\mu(s),$$ where $\mu$ is a positive (Bernstein) measure and $y>0$. This is the "hidden linear structure": $I$ is a linear functional of $\mu$.

The key equations. The dual problem picks a Lagrange multiplier $\eta$ (the shadow price of the budget) tied by the budget equation $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\xi\, I(\eta\,\xi)\right]=x_0,$$ where $\xi$ is the state-price density and $x_0$ the initial wealth. Now perturb preferences affinely, $\mu_\varepsilon=\mu+\varepsilon\,\nu$, with $\nu$ a signed perturbing measure and $\varepsilon$ small. Affineness of $\mu\mapsto I$ makes the dual value function affine in the induced quantities, and the multiplier becomes analytic: $$\eta(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\ge 0} a_n\,\varepsilon^{n},$$ with the coefficients $a_n$ given by explicit recursions built from Bell polynomials $B_{n,k}$ (which encode the derivatives of a composite function via Faà di Bruno's formula). These feed convergent expansions for the primal value function and the optimal terminal wealth.

The method. The toolkit is the Hausdorff–Bernstein–Widder representation of completely monotone functions, convex duality for the investment problem, the implicit-function/analyticity machinery to establish that $\eta(\varepsilon)$ is analytic, and Faà di Bruno's formula (hence Bell polynomials) to assemble the higher-order coefficients recursively.

Results with numbers. As a theorem-driven paper, the deliverable is not an error-vs-baseline number but a mathematical guarantee: arbitrary-order, convergent expansions with explicit recursive coefficients for the value function and optimal terminal wealth under affine preference perturbations.

Jargon Decoded

  • utility function — a mathematical description of how much satisfaction an investor derives from wealth, encoding risk aversion.
  • completely monotone function — a smooth function whose derivatives alternate in sign, representable as a mixture of decaying exponentials.
  • Bernstein representation — writing such a function as a continuous mixture of exponentials against a positive measure.
  • Lagrange multiplier — a scalar that enforces a constraint (here the budget) inside an optimization.
  • state-price density — the stochastic discount factor that prices every payoff in the market.
  • Bell polynomials — combinatorial polynomials that organize the higher-order derivatives of a composite function.
  • analyticity — the property of locally equalling one's own convergent power series.

Honest Take

This is pure theory: no market data, no realized P&L, and it lives inside two idealizations — CMIM utilities and stochastically dominant markets — while real markets aren't obviously stochastically dominant and impose transaction costs and trading constraints the model omits. What it delivers is genuinely useful as a foundation, though: the recursive Bell-polynomial coefficients are directly implementable, so a modeler can actually compute how sensitive an optimal strategy is to preference misspecification.

Bottom Line

Perturb the investor's preferences and the optimal portfolio follows a clean, computable power series — smooth dials, no cliffs.

4. Retained hidden excess generates memory in price-limited markets

Debraj Das
cond-mat.stat-mech · 2026-08-09 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

When an exchange caps how far a stock can move in a day, the part of the move it clips off doesn't vanish — a fraction of it secretly carries into the next day, manufacturing predictable momentum out of purely random shocks.

Plain English

The problem. Many exchanges impose daily price limits — a stock may move at most, say, ±10% before trading is halted at the band. The limits exist to curb panic, but they raise a puzzle. Suppose a stock "wants" to jump +15% today but is clipped at +10%: the extra +5% of buying pressure didn't evaporate — so where does it go, and does it make tomorrow's move predictable? This matters for risk management (limit-hit days cluster ominously) and for anyone modeling returns near the band. Everyday picture: a dam holding back a flood. The water you don't see spill over today is still pressing on the wall, ready to push tomorrow.

The idea. The authors build a deliberately minimal model. Each day an independent random shock hits the stock — the shocks themselves carry no memory. But the excess beyond the band gets partly retained: a fixed fraction of today's clipped-off amount is added to tomorrow's latent (true, pre-clip) return. That single retention mechanism is enough to generate genuine memory in the observed returns, even though the underlying noise is memoryless. It's the dam again: independent rainfalls, but a wall that holds back and carries forward the overflow.

What they found. Analytically, several clean predictions fall out. The long-run distribution of latent returns keeps the same tail index as the driving noise (equally fat-tailed) but with a larger tail amplitude — clipping-and-retention makes big moves more likely, not less. After a day that closes at a limit, the next day's mean return has the same sign and grows in proportion to the band width; the probability of hitting the same limit again settles to a finite value; while hitting the opposite limit next day is power-law suppressed (it needs a second, opposite extreme shock). Empirical data from real price-limited stocks are qualitatively consistent with this same-sign, momentum-like response and its growth with wider bands.

The Math

The setup. Let $r_t$ be the latent (unobserved, pre-clip) daily return and $x_t=\mathrm{clip}(r_t,[-b,b])$ the observed return, where $b>0$ is the band half-width. The clipped-off excess is $e_t=r_t-x_t$. The dynamics are the stochastic recurrence $$r_{t+1}=\eta_{t+1}+\kappa\,e_t,$$ where $\eta_{t+1}$ are i.i.d. symmetric shocks with regularly varying tails (tail index $\alpha$), and $\kappa\in[0,1)$ is the retained fraction. Independent $\eta$'s, but $\kappa e_t$ couples today to tomorrow.

The key equations. For regularly varying noise, the stationary latent return preserves the tail exponent, $$\mathbb{P}(|r|>u)\sim c\,u^{-\alpha}\quad(u\to\infty),$$ with the same $\alpha$ as $\eta$ but an enhanced amplitude $c$. In the wide-band limit, a close at $+b$ is described by a single dominant shock, giving a same-sign drift with $\mathbb{E}[x_{t+1}\mid \text{close at }+b]\propto b$, a finite recurrence probability $\mathbb{P}(\text{hit }+b\text{ again})\to p^\star$, and a power-law-suppressed $\mathbb{P}(\text{hit }-b)$. Here $u$ is a return level, $c$ the tail amplitude, $\alpha$ the tail index, $b$ the band, and $\kappa$ the retention.

The method. The analysis combines stochastic recurrence equation theory with regular variation (heavy-tail asymptotics) to get the stationary tail, plus a single-dominant-shock heuristic for the wide-band regime, all cross-checked with Monte Carlo simulation and an empirical comparison to real price-limited stocks.

Results with numbers. Tail index preserved, amplitude enhanced; post-limit next-day mean return same-sign and $\propto b$; same-limit recurrence probability $\to$ a finite constant; opposite-limit probability power-law suppressed; simulations confirm the analytics and real data are qualitatively consistent.

Jargon Decoded

  • price limit / band — an exchange-imposed maximum daily move, halting trades at $\pm b$.
  • latent state — an unobserved variable (here the pre-clip return) driving the observed data.
  • regularly varying tail — a distribution whose extreme values decay like a power law $u^{-\alpha}$.
  • tail index — the exponent $\alpha$ controlling how fat the distribution's tail is (smaller = fatter).
  • stochastic recurrence equation — a random update rule $r_{t+1}=f(\eta_{t+1},r_t)$ linking successive states.
  • stationary distribution — the long-run distribution the process settles into.

Honest Take

It's intentionally minimal: one retention parameter and symmetric shocks, whereas real markets have asymmetric responses, volume and liquidity effects, and strategic behavior clustering around the limits. The empirical support is "qualitatively consistent," not a fitted, out-of-sample forecast. But that minimalism is the point — it shows post-limit momentum can emerge from a single mechanical channel, no behavioral storytelling required, which is a clean null hypothesis worth having.

Bottom Line

The price move the exchange hides today doesn't disappear — a slice of it leaks into tomorrow, turning a safety rail into a momentum engine.

5. Nash Peer-to-Peer Insurance Bargaining under Price Fairness and Coalitional Stability

Tim J. Boonen, Wing Fung Chong, Kenneth Tsz Hin Ng, Tak Wa Ng
q-fin.RM · 2026-08-10 · abstract · pdf

The Gist

It models peer-to-peer insurance as a bargaining problem, deriving the fair contract that every member voluntarily accepts and that no breakaway subgroup can improve upon.

Plain English

The problem. In peer-to-peer (P2P) insurance, a group of people pool their risks and a P2P "reinsurer" backstops the big losses — instead of everyone buying policies from a traditional insurance company. Because everyone is risk-averse and participation is voluntary, the contract has to clear three bars at once: every member must end up better off than going it alone; the pricing must feel fair across members; and the arrangement must be stable, meaning no clique of members is tempted to walk out and form its own smaller pool. Everyday version: friends splitting the cancellation risk on a group trip. The way costs are shared has to feel fair, has to beat each person self-insuring, and shouldn't leave three of them muttering that they'd be better off breaking away on their own.

The idea. The authors cast this as asymmetric Nash bargaining: all parties jointly maximize the product of their gains in expected utility over their "walk-away" (disagreement) points, with weights reflecting bargaining power. They impose a price-fairness condition — each peer's expected contribution equals a common loading times that peer's own expected loss, so nobody is quietly overcharged relative to the risk they bring. They then justify axiomatically why the bargaining formulation is the right one (versus a fixed-weight weighted-sum objective), and, most practically, they derive checkable conditions guaranteeing coalitional stability — that no subgroup can do better by defecting.

What they found. They prove the optimal contract exists and is unique, and characterize it via first-order conditions across three regimes — full, partial, and zero reinsurance. They give computationally tractable sufficient conditions ruling out profitable subgroup deviations, both with and without price fairness. Numerically, two lessons stand out: price fairness compresses the dispersion in risk allocations and in each member's certainty-equivalent loading; and — counterintuitively — bigger pools do not always raise welfare. Growth helps through diversification but can hurt through shifting bargaining power, so "more members" is not automatically "better."

The Math

The setup. Peers $i=1,\dots,n$ face random losses $L_i$ and have risk-averse utilities $u_i$ with disagreement points $d_i$ (their expected utility if no deal is struck); a P2P reinsurer has utility $u_0$. A contract specifies each peer's indemnity $I_i(L_i)$ (how much of the loss is covered) and premium $\pi_i$.

The key equations. The asymmetric Nash bargaining solution maximizes the weighted product of gains: $$\max_{\{I_i,\pi_i\}}\;\prod_{i=0}^{n}\big(U_i-d_i\big)^{\gamma_i},$$ where $U_i$ is agent $i$'s expected utility under the contract, $d_i$ their disagreement point, and $\gamma_i>0$ their bargaining weight. Price fairness imposes, consistent with the expected-value premium principle, $$\pi_i=(1+\theta)\,\mathbb{E}[L_i],$$ with a common loading $\theta$ applied to every peer's expected loss $\mathbb{E}[L_i]$. First-order (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker) conditions then pin down the indemnity functions $I_i$ across the full-, partial-, and zero-reinsurance regimes. Coalitional stability requires that for every subgroup $S$, the value it could secure alone, $v(S)$, does not exceed what its members receive in the grand coalition — a core-type condition translated into checkable restrictions on the utilities.

The method. The tools are constrained convex optimization (maximizing the Nash product subject to fairness and participation), an axiomatic characterization justifying the bargaining objective, and cooperative game theory (core / coalitional-deviation analysis) to derive the stability conditions.

Results with numbers. Existence and uniqueness of the optimal contract; closed first-order characterizations in three reinsurance regimes; tractable sufficient conditions excluding coalitional deviations; and numerically, price fairness reduces dispersion in risk allocations and certainty-equivalent loadings, while welfare is non-monotone in pool size.

Jargon Decoded

  • peer-to-peer insurance — members pool their risks and share losses directly rather than through a traditional insurer.
  • Nash bargaining solution — the allocation maximizing the product of the players' gains over their walk-away points.
  • disagreement point — each party's outcome if no deal is reached.
  • expected-value premium principle — setting a premium as a loading multiplied by the expected loss.
  • coalitional stability / core — the property that no subgroup can do strictly better by breaking away.
  • reinsurance — insurance bought by an insurer (here the P2P pool) to cover its own large losses.
  • certainty equivalent — the guaranteed amount an agent would accept in place of a risky outcome.

Honest Take

The model runs on expected-utility agents and a single common loading, both simplifications — real pools face heterogeneous, hard-to-observe risk and moral hazard that this framework doesn't price. The coalitional-stability results are sufficient conditions, not necessary ones, so some real defection incentives could be missed or over-cautiously ruled out. Still, as a rigorous scaffold for designing small voluntary pools — and for understanding precisely when growing the pool stops helping — it's a genuinely useful contribution.

Bottom Line

Fair, voluntary, defection-proof insurance is fundamentally a bargaining problem — and a bigger pool is not automatically a better one.
